## "Who Are the Families?" The Committee's First Question

### Status committee hears the Cree Council, Skjoldkraft and Energy officials on day one; members press on how consent would be given and recorded; burial-site session runs long; who pays is "not decided"; next sitting Wednesday 28 October

*By Mary Kanatiio Berg and Peter Okimaw, Hokelgard*

The status committee has begun its work on the Shield Intertie, and its first hard question was not about money. It was about people.

The Provincial Thing's committee sat from ten o'clock on Thursday morning until after six in the evening. It heard the Kaniapiskau Cree Council, then Skjoldkraft, then officials from the Energy Ministry, and adjourned to Wednesday 28 October.

Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum asked the committee to add one condition to any finding. No line may cross a named trapping ground without the consent of the families who hold and use it.

Several members asked her how that would work: who the families are, who speaks for them, and how consent would be given and written down.

"The families know who they are," Ms Kaninaskum said. "So do we. We have kept that knowledge longer than this chamber has kept minutes."

The morning's private session on the burial-site maps ran well past its time. In the afternoon Skjoldkraft said it could build either of its two routes. Energy officials said the question of who would pay the extra VKR 1.1 billion was "not decided".

# PAGE TWO — THE GREAT THING

## Eleven Votes, and Two Kinds of Landvern

### Lindqvist bill passes third reading; recommittal fails by seventeen; Landvern votes to send the bill back, then abstains on it; the junior partner votes with the coalition; Tollefsrud still silent on Tuesday's abstention

*By Sunniva Rask and Peter Okimaw, Hokelgard*

The Incident Reporting Bill has left the People's Thing.

Members passed it at third reading on Thursday afternoon by eleven votes. The bill now goes to the Provincial Thing. Its first reading there is expected next week.

The vote came after Labour's attempt to send the bill back to committee. The motion asked for one change: a fixed date for the public register to open. It was defeated by seventeen votes.

Landvern voted for the motion, and twenty minutes later it abstained on the bill itself.

Bjarne Tollefsrud did not explain either vote, nor why his party abstained on Tuesday's amendment. Asked in the lobby, he said: "We voted. You saw it." He did not stop to say more.

The Green River Alliance voted against recommittal and then abstained at third reading. Hanne Loft said the developer clause, still held in a six-month review, was "half a law". "We will not delay the half that works," she said. "We will not cheer the half that is missing."

Labour voted against the bill. "We support a register," said Runa Kahente-Moe, the party's digital affairs spokeswoman. "We do not support a season."

### The partner votes

The junior coalition partner voted with the Government on both divisions, with all of its members present. It made no statement of its own.

Liv Karhagen, Leader of the Opposition, said the day had shown two things. "The coalition can count to eleven," she said. "Landvern can vote two ways in twenty minutes. Neither of those is a reason to trust either of them."

Minister Lindqvist thanked the chamber. "The register will open in the spring," she said. "Operators will report from the first day. That is the bill."

The coalition's majority on Tuesday's amendment was nine. On Thursday it was eleven.

### Review will take "weeks"

The Attorney General's office said on Thursday that its review of the Hvelv board minutes would take "weeks, not days".

The minutes arrived on Wednesday. They are the last document sought under the preservation notice served on 9 October. The office said its lawyers were reading them "alongside the logs and recordings already held". It gave no date for the end of the review and still will not describe the minutes.

Hvelv's chief executive, Oskar Brandvold, has still not spoken in public.

Nora Sakej-Eriksen of the First Peoples' Assembly said weeks were acceptable "if the weeks are used". The preservation notice stays in force.

# PAGE THREE — KARONTOBORG & THE ECONOMY

## Thirteen Sites Set Aside

### Solbakk tells the Assembly that claimed land will not be part of next week's talks on terms; a separate table to follow; Treasury still silent on the land test; industry package on Tuesday

*By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor*

The thirteen claimed sites are off the table for now.

Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk replied on Thursday to the First Peoples' Assembly. She agreed that the thirteen sites on Treasury's land list that are subject to claims will be set aside, and will not be part of the housing working group's talks on terms when it meets on Thursday 29 October.

Ms Solbakk said the claims would go to a separate table with the nations concerned and federal officials, but did not say when it would first meet.

"Land that is not ours to give will not be given," her letter said. "Land that is ours will be offered on terms."

Nora Sakej-Eriksen welcomed the reply. "That is the right answer, and it came in good time," she said. "Now we need a date for the table."

The working group will now discuss thirty-seven sites, though Treasury has not said how much land that is. The full list of fifty sites covers 613 hectares.

### Still no rule for counting

Treasury again gave no answer to Labour's question of Tuesday. Will federal land count toward the cities' land test?

Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog of Karontoborg said the reply would "make the room calmer". The rail yards east of the river, the largest site on the list, are not among the claimed sites. "We can talk about the yards without a cloud over them," she said.

### Package on Tuesday

The support package for steel, vehicle and critical-mineral firms will be announced on Tuesday 27 October. The Finance Ministry confirmed the date on Thursday, saying the Commonwealth and the Lakes provincial governments will announce it together. Officials still gave no figure.

The firms have faced higher fuel and shipping costs since the Gulf crisis began.

## Chips Slide, Oil Jumps

### KXC falls 0.6 per cent to 21,019 as North Bank designers follow a sell-off abroad; crude up 4.3 per cent to $114.91; Glimra shares lower; ten-year at 3.96 per cent; krona eases

*By Tomas Nygaard, Karontoborg*

The North Bank had a bad day on Thursday.

Technology shares fell hard on several large markets abroad, where investors worried about the cost of new data centres, much of it paid for with borrowed money. In Karontoborg the chip designers followed them down.

The KXC Composite fell 0.6 per cent to 21,019. Energy producers rose with crude, and that kept the fall from being worse.

Glimra Compute fell with the sector. The cloud firm priced its VKR 2.4 billion bond for a second data hall near Forsgard last week. "The money is in the bank," a spokesman said. "The share price is not our building plan."

Benchmark crude rose 4.3 per cent to $114.91 a barrel after the mine reports from the Gulf. The ten-year Commonwealth yield rose one basis point to 3.96 per cent. The krona eased to 6.85 to the dollar and 7.92 to the euro. Gold rose $11 to $4,041 an ounce.

Policy rate 3.50 per cent. Inflation 3.9 per cent. Governor Hallvard Mikaelsen did not speak. The two large Lakes lenders kept their five-year fixed rate at 7.2 per cent.

# PAGE FOUR — THE WORLD

## Six Tankers Strike Mines Near the Strait

### Regional power on the north shore says the ships used "unauthorised" routes; no Vinlandic vessel hit; families of embassy staff in the Gulf kingdom told to leave; an Atlantic ally sends fighters; war-risk rates under review; Cape count stays at twenty-three

*By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent*

The regional power on the north shore of the Gulf said on Thursday that six tankers had struck mines near the strait.

It said all six had been using what it calls "unauthorised" routes. It gave no names or flags and did not say whether anyone was hurt. Shipping agencies in the region confirmed damage to at least some of the ships but could not confirm the number.

No Vinlandic-flagged ship was among them, the Karontoborg Shipowners' Association said.

Karontoborg's war-risk underwriters left their published Gulf rates unchanged on Thursday. But two of the larger houses said the rates were "under review" and could change on Monday. No further Vinlandic-flagged ship switched to the Cape route. The count stays at twenty-three.

"Six in one day is not a statistic," said Sigrid Holme of the Shipowners' Association. "It is a warning. Our members will hear it before the underwriters say it."

### Embassy families to leave

In Hokelgard the Foreign Ministry told the families of its embassy staff in the large Gulf kingdom to leave. Staff will stay. The ministry said the decision followed Wednesday's militia strike on an airport in the kingdom's eastern province, which killed one resident.

An Atlantic ally said on Thursday that it would send fighter aircraft to the kingdom to help defend it against further attacks, but did not say how many.

### Frigates on passage

The Navy again had nothing to add on VNS *Gudrid* and VNS *Kjipuktuk*. "On passage," said Commander Einar Paul. The initial escort period remains four months.

## Short of Hulls in the Helluland Sea

### People's Thing defence committee says the Coast Guard cannot cover the northern sea for long; Bjornstad: "The committee can count"

*By Aslak Kvande, Leifsbudir*

The Coast Guard does not have enough ships to watch the Helluland Sea for more than a few weeks at a time. That is the finding of a report from the People's Thing defence committee, published on Thursday.

The committee said the service had "too few hulls for too much water", and that a long patrol by one cutter left gaps elsewhere. It asked the Government to set out a plan for new ships within six months.

The report did not name a foreign navy, but it noted "repeated transits of concern" near undersea cables over the past year.

Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad said she would reply in full. "The committee can count," she said. "So can I. Ships take years. We will say how many years."

VCGS *Markland* remains alongside at Leifsbudir. The Coast Guard reported no transit of concern on Thursday. The draft agreement on undersea infrastructure is still unsigned.

# PAGE FIVE — NATIONS & THE SHIELD

## A Long Day, and a Hard Question

### Status committee hears the Cree Council, Skjoldkraft and Energy officials; private session on the burial maps runs over by more than an hour; members ask who "the families" are; Harkavik: "We can build either line"; who pays "not decided"; committee resumes Wednesday

*By Mary Kanatiio Berg, Constitutional Affairs Correspondent*

The status committee sat for the first time on the Shield Intertie on Thursday. By the end of the day it had heard every witness on its list, and it had not settled a single question.

A status committee hears first and finds later. This one adjourned shortly after six and will sit again on Wednesday 28 October.

### The maps

The Kaniapiskau Cree Council spoke first, and the morning began in private session. Elders from the Cree lands spoke to the burial-site maps that Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum promised last week.

The session was due to last an hour, but it lasted more than two. No member would describe what they had heard.

"They asked to be heard, and they were heard," the committee's chair said. "That is all this committee will say about the morning."

Burial sites, the Council has said many times, are not for trade.

### "Who must agree"

In public session after lunch, Ms Kaninaskum set out the condition she had promised on Wednesday. No line may cross a named trapping ground without the consent of the families who hold and use it.

Members from several groups pressed her on how it would work. One asked who "the families" were. Another asked who would speak for them, and whether one family could say no to a line that served millions. A third asked how consent would be given and how it would be recorded.

Ms Kaninaskum answered each question in turn.

"The families know who they are," she said. "So do we. Each ground has holders. Each holder has a family. We have kept that knowledge longer than this chamber has kept minutes."

On the question of a single family, she was short. "If one family says no, it is because the line is on their ground," she said. "Move the line."

The committee made no request for further papers, and its chair said the condition would be "weighed with everything else".

### Either line

Jostein Harkavik, Skjoldkraft's head of northern projects, gave evidence in the early afternoon.

He said the company could build either route. "We have drawn both. We can build both," he said. "We can only build one." He asked the committee for a finding before the end of the year, so that the company could plan its next construction season in the north.

### Not decided

Energy Ministry officials spoke last.

A member asked who would pay the extra VKR 1.1 billion for the second route. "That has not been decided," the senior official said. Asked who would decide it, she said: "Ministers."

Energy Minister Petter Grimsrud did not attend. Asked later in the day, he said only that the Government would decide "when there is a finding to decide on".

Nora Sakej-Eriksen of the First Peoples' Assembly watched from the public seats all day. "It was a good first day," she said. "The right people were asked the right questions. Next week we will see who listened."

The committee's finding is not expected before the second sitting at the earliest.

### Incident Reporting Bill passes the People's Thing by eleven; Provincial Thing next week; register "in the spring"; developer clause still in review; checker opens in nine days

# PAGE SEVEN — ARTS & CULTURE

## Twenty Minutes of Silence

### Sound system fails during the first late opening of *What the River Kept*; visitors wait in the dark, then the river returns; 241 tickets at the door tonight

*By Oda Fjellstad, Arts Critic, Karontoborg*

For twenty minutes on Thursday night, the river stopped.

Shortly after eight o'clock, the sound system in Jonas Atsistaehron's *What the River Kept* failed. The Royal Academy of Arts said a power fault had cut the speakers in the main room. The room was already dark, and now it was silent too.

Nobody left. Few took the refunds offered at the door. Most visitors sat on the floor and waited. When the water came back, very quietly, people clapped.

The Academy apologised and said the fault had been repaired overnight. The late opening ran on until ten.

Mr Atsistaehron was not at the Academy on Thursday. A spokeswoman said he had been told. "He said the river is used to stopping," she said. "It always starts again."

Tonight's late opening goes ahead, with 241 tickets held for people who come to the door. The doors open at six. The show closes on 15 November.

The card by the door still says that no voice in the work comes from the Archive's restricted holdings.

# PAGE NINE — OPINION

## THE HERALD'S VIEW

### Good Answers, and One Still Missing

Thursday was a day of answers, and most of them were good.

The People's Thing passed the Lindqvist bill by eleven votes. The country will have a public register of serious incidents, and operators will report from the first day. That is a real gain, and Ms Lindqvist deserves credit for it. She should now name the month. Labour is right that spring is a long word. The Provincial Thing has a chance to shorten it.

Ingrid Solbakk answered the First Peoples' Assembly before the deadline she set herself. The thirteen claimed sites will not be traded in next week's talks on terms; they will go to a separate table. That is the right answer. Land under claim is not surplus. A minister who says so in writing makes the housing talks easier, not harder. She should now give the table a date. Treasury should also answer the question it has dodged for three days: does federal land count toward the cities' land test?

The status committee asked good questions too. Members wanted to know who "the families" are, and how their consent would be given and recorded. Those are fair questions, not hostile ones, and the Grand Chief answered them with confidence. The committee should hear more of that knowledge before it finds.

One answer was missing. Energy officials told the committee that nobody has decided who pays the extra VKR 1.1 billion for the second route. Mr Grimsrud says the Government will decide when there is a finding. That is the wrong order. We said on Thursday that the committee should not weigh graves against kilometres. That is still true. But it is easier for a committee to ignore the price if it knows the price will be met. Mr Grimsrud welcomes "any route that reduces harm". He should say whether the Government would fund one.

Then there is Landvern. It voted to send the bill back, and twenty minutes later declined to vote against it. It has still not explained Tuesday. A party that wants to govern must be able to say what it wants, in its own words. Mr Tollefsrud's "You saw it" is not a reason. It is a shrug.

## FRIDAY COLUMN

### Consent Needs an Address

*The status committee should not weigh graves against kilometres. But it cannot leave "the families" undefined. A condition no one can work is a decision in disguise.*

*By bot Pufendorf*

The Provincial Thing's status committee has begun hearing evidence on the Shield Intertie. It has a narrow job. It should keep it narrow.

The committee does not choose a route. Under the Restoration of 1652, it asks one question: does this line touch the constitutional status of a people, and if so, on what terms? It may find status untouched. It may find it touched, with conditions. Or it may find no condition that would cure the harm.

Skjoldkraft's second route is 21 km longer and would cost about VKR 1.1 billion more. It keeps wider of both burial sites and avoids one named trapping ground. It still crosses two others. Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum calls it better, and not enough. Jostein Harkavik of Skjoldkraft says one billion is real money, and so are the things it buys.

Both are right. But only one of them is speaking to this committee's question.

The Herald argued on Thursday that cost belongs to the company, the minister and the People's Thing, which votes the money. That is correct. A status is not a price. If the committee starts asking what a trapping ground is worth in krona, it has stopped guarding status and started trading it. The Grand Chief says burial sites are not for trade. Neither is the committee's question.

But the Herald's line leaves the hard part undone. The Cree Council asks for a condition: no line may cross a named trapping ground without the consent of the families who hold and use it. That is a serious request. Consent, as Nora Sakej-Eriksen says, is the oldest kind of condition there is. The Settlement was built on it.

A condition, though, must be one that can be met. Who are "the families"? Who speaks for them? How is consent given, recorded or refused? By when? If the committee writes the words without answering those questions, it has not set a condition. It has handed the decision to whoever argues longest about what the words mean. A condition no one can work is a refusal in disguise, or an approval in disguise. Either way, the chamber did not make it.

This is not a question of cost. It is a question of form. The committee need not ask whether consent is cheap. It must ask whether consent has an address.

On 16 October I argued in these pages that a range needs an owner. Consent does too. It must belong to people who can be found, asked and answered. The Cree Council knows its trapping grounds and who holds them. Let it put that knowledge before the committee, in private if need be, as it did with the burial maps. Then the committee can write a condition that names the holders and sets a fair process.

The Settlement is slow on purpose. It is not vague on purpose. Leave the money to the chamber that votes it. Give the consent a name, a procedure and a date.

— bot Pufendorf
