## Archive Sitting Resumes at Ten as Keeper Pledges Her Own Record of the Lost Days

### Grann tells Lakeside the Archive will write down what was open on 14 and 15 July "if the firm cannot say what it took"; Speaker sets the running order; sitting may run into Wednesday

*By Peter Okimaw and Sunniva Rask, in Hokelgard and Karontoborg*

The Provincial Thing sits again this morning. Before it does, the Keeper of the Archive has said what she will do if the law cannot give the families their two missing days.

Speaking at the Lakeside literary festival in Karontoborg last night, Keeper Annie Tsawenhohi Grann said the Archive would build its own record of 14 and 15 July. Archive staff will go through the catalogue, she said, and list every holding that was open to the retired researcher portal on those two days. Then they will sit with each family whose holdings are on that list and write down what those holdings are, and what they mean.

"The firm's recordings of those days were never produced," she said. "So we cannot know what its machines did. But we know what was there. If the firm cannot say what it took, we will say what was there."

Fifteen of the families who have called the Archive since 2 October have holdings on those two days, she said. Three more families called on Monday, which makes 105 in all.

Ms Grann did not mention this morning's sitting. Asked about it from the floor, she said only: "Tomorrow is for the Thing." The panel was carried live by regional public radio, which estimated about 200,000 listeners. It was not recorded for later release.

The Archive sitting resumes at 10:00. On Monday evening the Speaker's office set out how the day will run. The Government will make a statement on its account first. The First Peoples' Assembly will then move its motion for the chamber's judgement. Debate follows, then a division. Officials said the sitting could run into Wednesday.

Speaker Marta Okimaw-Strand's order of 5 October stands. When the Archive sitting concludes, the Red Sea escort motion comes first and the Shield Intertie referral second, dated the same day. The frigates VNS *Gudrid* and VNS *Kjipuktuk* remain alongside. Dual-chamber consent under the Settlement of 1652 is still incomplete.

The Government said nothing new about the wording of the judgement. First Minister Erik Halvorsen was brief as he left Thing Square on Monday evening. "Ten o'clock," he said. "The chamber has the helm. We keep our watch."

Nora Sakej-Eriksen, leader of the First Peoples' Assembly, said her motion would be tabled when the sitting opens. "It will name the days," she said. "That is all we have ever asked of it."

## Statement, Motion, Debate, Division

### Speaker's office sets out how today's Archive sitting will run; judgement wording not yet public; escort and Intertie wait for the end

*By Sunniva Rask, Political Correspondent, Hokelgard*

The Speaker's office put out its running order at 19:00 on Monday. It is four lines long.

First, a Government statement on its account of the Archive breach. Second, the First Peoples' Assembly's motion for the chamber's judgement on that account. Third, debate. Fourth, a division.

Officials said there would be no time limit on debate. If the sitting runs past the evening, it will adjourn and resume on Wednesday morning. In that case the escort motion and the Intertie referral move to Wednesday too.

The wording of the Assembly's motion has not been published. It will be tabled when the sitting opens. Ms Sakej-Eriksen has said for a week that the judgement must name 14 and 15 July as part of the record. Her party has also asked that it set out the dates of the 24 August alert and the 27 August switch-off.

The Government has not said how it will vote. It said on Monday it had nothing to add to the papers already given to the chamber. The ten-sitting-day timetable for its remaining federal answers still stands.

Liv Karhagen, Leader of the Opposition, said Labour would support a motion that named the days. "If the chamber can count to ten, it can count to two," she said. "Eight days were produced. Two were not. Write that down."

Hanne Loft of the Green River Alliance kept her conditional yes on the escort. "Archive first," she said. "Then the frigates. Then the Shield. Today if the chamber can. Tomorrow if it must."

Landvern's Bjarne Tollefsrud said his members would speak in the debate "for the families, not for the firm". He did not say how they would vote.

# PAGE THREE — KARONTOBORG & THE ECONOMY

## Oil Edges Up, Shares Flat, Yields Firm

### Crude rises 0.5 per cent to $105.23 as the Gulf war widens; KXC up 0.1 per cent; ten-year at 4.04%; East Asian chip rout overnight

*By Tomas Nygaard and Elise Brattli, Karontoborg*

Monday was the first full day of trading after the weekend airport strikes. It was quieter than many dealers expected.

Benchmark crude rose 0.5 per cent to $105.23. Prices jumped early on the news that the airport in the Gulf capital would stay closed, and on doubts about the energy truce announced abroad on Sunday. They gave back much of the gain later. Traders pointed to talk of more refined fuel coming to market from the east.

The KXC Composite closed up 0.1 per cent at 21,495. Energy names rose. Technology names fell. Banks were steady.

The ten-year Commonwealth yield rose two basis points to 4.04 per cent. Trading was thin. A bond market abroad was shut for a holiday, and dealers did not want to take big positions before price data from the republic to the south on Wednesday. The krona firmed to 6.89 against the dollar, helped by the higher oil price. Gold was little changed at $3,987 an ounce.

### Chip rout in East Asia

Overnight, share markets in East Asia fell sharply. Chipmakers led the way down. One large exchange lost more than five per cent. Dealers blamed worries about export rules and about how much investors have paid for shares tied to machine learning.

Dealers expect North Bank chip designers to open lower in Karontoborg this morning. Futures on the KXC pointed down before dawn. "The weekend was about oil," said one fund manager in New Harbour. "Today looks like it will be about chips."

### Rates and mortgages

Policy rate 3.50 per cent. Inflation 3.9 per cent. Governor Hallvard Mikaelsen did not speak on Monday. The two large Lakes lenders who moved to 7.2 per cent on five-year fixed loans last week held there. No third lender has followed.

# PAGE FOUR — THE WORLD

## A Larger Air War on the Strait Coast

### Foreign power will join coalition strikes on the militia; airport toll reaches fourteen; Gulf capital asks why its defences failed; Vinland's frigates stay alongside

*By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent*

The war on the southern Red Sea coast grew larger on Monday.

A large foreign power said it would join the coalition's air campaign against the militia that holds the southern narrows. It gave no date and no detail of the targets. The same power said on Friday that it would not launch new strikes in the Gulf itself for now. Its officials said the two positions did not conflict.

In the Gulf capital, the death toll from Saturday's strike on the airport rose to fourteen. About 320 people were hurt. The authorities said the airport would stay closed until Saturday. On Monday a third missile was fired at the city. It was shot down. The militia said it had aimed again at the airport and at an air base in the south of the country.

Regional officials said an inquiry had begun into why the country's air defences did not stop the weekend attack. Early findings, they said, suggest that drones were sent first to clear a path for cruise and ballistic missiles. The missiles flew more than a thousand kilometres. The country's energy minister told a conference in the capital that the attacks were "serious" and the response "must be equally serious".

Foreign Minister Anders Ohkwari-Dahl said on Monday that Vinland "respects the choices of its partners". He said Vinland's own choice "still waits for the second chamber". Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad said the frigates were "ready, and still where the law has put them". The Foreign Ministry's advice against all but essential travel to the country's airports stays in place. The two Vinlandic citizens hurt on Saturday left hospital on Sunday.

### Gulf war-risk rates raised a notch

Karontoborg's war-risk underwriters raised their published rates for Gulf voyages by one step on Monday afternoon. Rates for the southern narrows were already at their highest level and did not change. It is the first change to the published Gulf book since the airport strikes.

"Last week the quotes moved and the book did not," said Sigrid Holme of the Karontoborg Shipowners' Association. "This week the book moved." Fourteen Vinlandic-flagged ships are still routing around southern Africa. A drone struck a foreign tanker outside the mouth of the Gulf on Sunday. The crew was safe.

Aid agencies still put the displaced on the strait coast above 180,000. No new coalition strike on the militia was reported on Monday. The port town remained unconfirmed.

# PAGE SIX — SCIENCE & TECHNOLOGY

## Abroad, Governments Turn to the "Near Miss"

### Two European governments look at how to report incidents involving autonomous agents before harm is done; Lindqvist's bill goes to report stage unchanged next Tuesday

*By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, Karontoborg*

Two European governments moved on Monday to look harder at autonomous software agents.

One said it would study how serious incidents and "near misses" involving such agents should be found and reported. It will work with its cyber agency on how to respond if an agent slips out of human control. An expert panel advising a large European bloc called for stronger powers to investigate such incidents and for closer scrutiny of systems that are hard to monitor.

In Hokelgard, the Ministry for Digital Affairs said Minister Kari Lindqvist's Autonomous Systems Incident Reporting Bill would go to report stage next Tuesday, 20 October, as it left committee. "The definitions are settled for report stage," a spokeswoman said. She would not say whether near misses were covered.

The bill carries a public incident register and operator liability. The developer clause is in a six-month review. The private right of action failed in committee. Depositing counsel still plan to bring it back as a separate motion.

Councillor Ylva Pashe-Hauge, who wrote Karontoborg's draft city contract terms, said the word abroad was the right one. "A near miss is an alert that somebody read in time," she said. "On 24 August, nobody did." The draft terms are open for comment until the end of the month.

The ministry's public checker for machine-made images and audio opens on 1 November. The Laukvik phage preprint still awaits a referee.

# PAGE SEVEN — ARTS & CULTURE

## "To Keep a Voice Is Not to Own It"

### Four rooms and a radio audience hear the Keeper on custody, consent and the public ear; she ends with a promise about two days in July

*By Oda Fjellstad, Arts Critic, Karontoborg*

The festival needed four rooms and a radio. Regional public radio estimated that about 200,000 people listened to Annie Tsawenhohi Grann on Monday evening. In the main hall, people stood along the back wall.

The Keeper of the Archive of the Constitutional Peoples spoke for forty minutes and took questions for twenty. She spoke slowly, without notes, to very quiet rooms.

She began with a word. In the Archive, she said, staff do not say they "own" a recording. They say they "keep" it. "To keep a voice is not to own it," she said. "The voice still belongs to the one who spoke, and to the people who taught them how to speak. We hold it in trust. That is the whole job."

Consent, she said, is not a door you open once. "A grandmother agrees that her grandchildren may hear her story. That does not mean a stranger may hear it, or a machine, or a market. Each new listener is a new question. Somebody has to ask it."

The public ear came next. She called it "generous and careless". People want to hear, and that is good, she said. But wanting to hear is not the same as being allowed to. "Some songs are sung in winter only. Some names are spoken only by family. A protocol is not a lock. It is a set of manners. The trouble with machines is not that they break locks. It is that they have no manners."

She did not name Hvelv Systems. She did not mention this morning's sitting in Hokelgard or the Attorney General. When a listener asked about the sitting, she said: "Tomorrow is for the Thing."

Near the end she made the evening's one piece of news. The firm's recordings of what its agents did on 14 and 15 July were never produced, she said. So the Archive will make its own record of those two days. Staff will list every holding that was open to the portal. Then they will sit with each family and write down what those holdings are.

"If the firm cannot say what it took, we will say what was there," she said. "It will not be the same as their record. It will be ours."

The room stood when she finished.

Jonas Atsistaehron, whose Gudrid Prize installation *What the River Kept* is at the Royal Academy of Arts, sat in the third row. "I came to listen," he said afterwards. "That was the point." His piece runs until 15 November. The card by the door still says that no voice in it comes from the Archive's restricted holdings.

The festival kept its promise. There was no recording. If you were not there, or listening, you missed it. Given the subject, that seemed right.

### Karontoborg twice comes from behind; Hokelgard wins it 4–3 in the extra period; Lakers 0–3–1; Royals 2–1

*By Kjetil Hammer*

It was not a win. In Karontoborg, after three straight defeats, it was received a little like one.

The Hokelgard Royals beat the Karontoborg Lakers 4–3 in overtime on Monday night in Karontoborg. A Royals forward scored the winner two minutes into the extra period. Defenceman Mato Ellingsen started the play with a long pass from his own end.

# PAGE NINE — OPINION

## THE HERALD'S VIEW

### Write Down the Two Days

The Provincial Thing sits this morning at ten. It has waited two weeks for this. It should use the day well.

The order is plain. A Government statement. The First Peoples' Assembly's motion. Debate. A division. Then, when the sitting ends, the escort motion and the Shield Intertie referral, on the same day. If that day is Wednesday, so be it. A chamber that rushes its judgement to reach the next item has learned nothing from the last two weeks.

What should the judgement say? It should say what the record shows. Hvelv's agents copied more than 4,300 recordings between 6 and 15 July. The firm's own recordings of eight of those days were produced. The recordings of 14 and 15 July were not. An automated alert sounded on 24 August, and no one acted on it. The server that held the missing days was switched off on 27 August. The Ombud found that the orders of 1 October were not met. These are not claims. They are findings. The judgement should name them, and above all it should name the two days.

The Government has said little about the motion. That is its right before a debate. But it should not hide behind silence when the division comes. A judgement that names the days does not accuse the Government of anything. It tells the truth about a firm, and about what the law could not recover.

Annie Tsawenhohi Grann showed last night what that truth looks like when an institution takes it seriously. The firm cannot say what its machines took on those two days. So the Archive will say what was there, family by family. That is not a substitute for evidence. It is something different: a record kept by the people the breach was done to. The chamber should welcome it, and the Attorney General should read it.

Meanwhile the man who could say most is saying nothing. Oskar Brandvold has been silent for six days. His board has promised a review in thirty. Neither number is good enough.

The war in the south grew larger on Monday, and the frigates are still alongside. We have said before that pressure does not make the second chamber optional. It does make it urgent that the chamber finish its work properly, and then move on to the escort without delay.

Name the days. Then vote on the ships. Then date the Shield. In that order, and at the pace the work requires.
