Deadline Day Dawns With Hvelv’s Last Two Days Still Missing

No new filing on Tuesday; firm says recovery is “continuing” and promises the recordings before noon; Ombud will publish his finding by five o’clock; Speaker will read it before she sets any date

By Peter Okimaw and Halldor Vik, in Hokelgard

The Privacy Ombud’s production window closes at noon today. Hvelv Systems still owes him two days of evidence.

Hvelv filed nothing on Tuesday. The session recordings for 14 and 15 July, the last two days of the breach, are still missing. The firm says they sit on a contract server that was switched off in August.

“Recovery is continuing,” a Hvelv spokeswoman said on Tuesday evening. “We expect to file before the window closes.” She would not say how much had been recovered, or why the server was switched off. Chief executive Oskar Brandvold did not comment.

The Ombud’s office spent Tuesday testing the custody chain against the recordings already filed. It gave no view on what it found. Officials said Ombud Henrik Salo would publish his finding on completeness by 17:00 today, whether or not the last files arrive. They would not say what he would do if they did not.

Annie Tsawenhohi Grann, Keeper of the Archive of the Constitutional Peoples, said nineteen more families had called on Tuesday. That makes seventy-seven since Friday. “They are not asking about servers,” she said. “They are asking whether their grandmothers’ voices went to war.”

The office of Speaker Marta Okimaw-Strand said she would read the Ombud’s report in full before deciding anything. It set no date for the Provincial Thing’s Archive sitting to resume. Monday’s order stands: escort motion first, Shield Intertie referral second.

That keeps the frigates VNS Gudrid and VNS Kjipuktuk alongside. The Provincial Thing has not voted on the escort. Under the Settlement of 1652, dual-chamber consent is still incomplete.

First Minister Erik Halvorsen kept it short at Thing Square. “Noon is noon,” he said. “That is the question before us. A pilot does not move the rocks to suit the ship.”

Liv Karhagen, Leader of the Opposition, said: “A deadline is not a target. It is the law.”

Militia Denies Retreat as Strait Fighting Moves Inland

Both sides count heavy losses; strikes claimed on airports at dawn; crude ends flat after an early fall; insurers hold their quotes

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor, and Aslak Kvande

The militia on the southern Red Sea coast denied on Tuesday that it had lost the narrows shore. The regional government said it had. Neither claim could be checked.

What is clear is that the war has spread inland. Fighting moved into the hills around a highland city to the east. Sources on both sides said more than 270 fighters had been killed in a single day. At dawn on Wednesday, the militia said it had fired drones and missiles at airports on both sides of the border.

Benchmark crude fell as much as 3 per cent early on Tuesday, then recovered. It closed at $105.46, up 0.1 per cent. Karontoborg insurers kept war-risk quotes where Monday had left them. Fourteen Vinlandic-flagged ships are still routing around southern Africa.

A Quiet Tuesday Before a Loud Noon

Ombud tests the chain against the tapes; Hvelv recovers in silence; families of 12 and 13 July hear first answers

By Peter Okimaw, Political Correspondent, Hokelgard

Tuesday was the last full day of the Ombud’s window. Hvelv spent it in silence.

No filing was lodged. The firm’s only public word came in the evening, in two sentences.

Inside the Ombud’s office, staff compared the custody records, which run through all ten days from 6 to 15 July, with the recordings for the first eight. They would not say whether the two matched.

In the Old Quarter, the Archive began to give families of 12 and 13 July their first answers. Ms Grann said her staff could now tell some callers which systems had held their relatives’ recordings. “Some of those answers are hard to hear,” she said. She would not say how many had been told that Cyber Command systems were involved.

For families of 14 and 15 July, there is still nothing to tell. “We have a record that the recordings moved,” Ms Grann said. “We do not have the recordings. A receipt is not a parcel.”

Officials in the Ombud’s office said the question of when and why the server was turned off “will form part of the finding”.

The First Minister’s ten-sitting-day timetable for the remaining federal answers still stands. Nora Sakej-Eriksen of the First Peoples’ Assembly said: “Two days were missing on Monday. Two days were missing on Tuesday. We will see what noon brings.”

Free Thing Reports Strain in the Coalition; Whips Deny It

Rival paper says the junior partner wants assurances on the escort vote; Herald cannot confirm; majority “holds”, say whips

By Sunniva Rask, Political Correspondent, Hokelgard

The Free Thing reported on Tuesday that the junior coalition partner had asked for a private meeting with the First Minister. It said the partner wanted assurances that the Government would not press the Provincial Thing to vote on the escort before the Archive sitting concludes.

The Herald could not confirm the report. A senior Commonwealth whip called it “a story about a meeting that has not happened”. He said the majority “holds, as it held yesterday”. The junior partner remains unnamed. No paper has named it.

Landvern leader Bjarne Tollefsrud saw “a Government waiting for a server and arguing with itself”. Hanne Loft of the Green River Alliance said her conditional yes had not changed. “My condition is the Archive,” she said.

Oil Swings and Settles; Yields Ease

Crude ends flat after reserve-release selling fades; ten-year slips four points with yields abroad; KXC edges up; krona firmer

By Tomas Nygaard and Elise Brattli, Karontoborg

The energy desks had a busy morning and a quiet close.

Benchmark crude fell as much as 3 per cent in early trade on Tuesday. Traders sold on the diesel reserves European partners agreed to release on Friday, and on reports that Gulf exports were rising. By the afternoon, the strait coast had pulled prices back. Crude closed at $105.46, up 0.1 per cent.

“The reserves are a cushion,” one desk said. “The strait is a wall. The market keeps hitting both.”

Bond markets were calmer. The ten-year Commonwealth yield fell four basis points to 4.09 per cent. It followed yields abroad, which eased from multi-year highs. The krona firmed to 6.83 against the dollar.

The KXC Composite rose 0.1 per cent to 21,641. Banks and homebuilders won back some of Monday’s losses; Lakeshore Homes rose 0.8 per cent. North Bank technology names were steady.

Sigrid Holme of the Karontoborg Shipowners’ Association said the price board did not change the routing. “The ships go round the Cape until the strait is safe and the Thing has spoken,” she said.

Mayors Do the Sum Solbakk Has Not

Eleven-city campaign says the cities will fall 60,000 homes short of three-year targets; borrowing relief “covers the foundations, not the house”

By Tomas Nygaard, Karontoborg

On Monday, Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk put a number on borrowing relief. On Tuesday, the mayors put a number on the gap.

The eleven-city housing campaign published its own supply arithmetic in Laukvik. It said the cities had planned 180,000 housing starts over three years. On current permits and current lending, they are on course for about 120,000. That leaves 60,000 homes short.

The campaign said Ms Solbakk’s working figure of up to VKR 6 billion in borrowing relief would close about a quarter of that gap at most. “It covers the foundations, not the house,” said Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie of Laukvik.

Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog of Karontoborg called it “the arithmetic Kaupang has been too polite to publish”. The cities want supply targets in the compact itself.

The Finance Ministry said its study was still under way. Ms Solbakk’s figure remains a working number, not a commitment and not a line in a bill.

Five-year fixed mortgage quotes still sit near 7 per cent. The policy rate is 3.50 per cent and inflation is 3.9 per cent.

Strait War Spreads Into the Hills

Militia says it still holds the coast; government says it has taken it; fighting inland kills hundreds; dawn strikes on airports

By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent

On Monday the regional government on the southern Red Sea coast said it had retaken most of the narrows shore. On Tuesday the militia said that was not true.

Its spokesman said its fighters still held all the ground they seized last month. Government military sources were more careful than their ministry. They said they had cut the supply road to the port town, but would not confirm it had fallen.

The heaviest fighting moved inland, to the hills around a highland city to the east. Sources on both sides said more than 270 fighters had died in a single day. Aid agencies said more than 180,000 people had now fled their homes.

At dawn on Wednesday the militia said it had fired drones and missiles at airports in the neighbouring kingdom and in the government-held south. There was no independent confirmation.

In Hokelgard, the escort remains a question for the second chamber. Foreign Minister Anders Ohkwari-Dahl told ASTO counterparts on Tuesday that the Provincial Thing’s order of business was unchanged.

Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad said the frigates were ready. “Whatever the map says this morning, it will say something else by the time we sail,” she said. “We plan for both.”

Seventh Arrest in Plot Against Allied Air Base

ASTO partner’s police hold a young man over a suspected state-backed drone plan; Vinland backs grey-zone paper

By Aslak Kvande, Leifsbudir

Police in an ASTO partner state made a seventh arrest on Tuesday in a suspected plot to attack an air base used by allied bombers. The suspect, a man of twenty-two, was held on suspicion of preparing an act of terrorism. Investigators believe the plot was backed by a foreign state. They have not said which.

Officials in Hokelgard said there was no known threat to Vinlandic bases. The case adds weight to the alliance paper on “grey-zone pressure”: drones near bases, jamming and cable tampering. Mr Ohkwari-Dahl said Vinland still supported the paper. He still wants it to name state survey ships. The undersea draft communiqué remains unsigned.

The Helluland Sea watch area was quiet on Tuesday. The cutter VCGS Markland remains alongside in Leifsbudir.

Airports Add Health Checks After Fever Death Abroad

East African state records first death from outbreak spreading from central Africa; Vinland adds screening for arrivals from the region

By Gunnhild Paquette, Science Correspondent

An East African state recorded its first death on Tuesday from a haemorrhagic fever outbreak that began in central Africa. The patient had travelled by road and air while ill. The strain has no approved vaccine or treatment.

The Commonwealth Public Health Agency said it would add health questions and temperature checks for passengers arriving from the region at Karontoborg and Hokelgard airports. It said the risk to Vinland “remains very low”. There are no direct flights from the affected countries.

In western Europe, more than a quarter of a million people marched on Tuesday in support of school pupils protesting over crowded classrooms. Police there used tear gas, and secondary-school classes were suspended for the week.

Energy Ministry “Would Not Object” to Same-Day Referral

Grimsrud’s office answers Kaninaskum’s condition; Speaker’s office says the date is the chamber’s to set; Cree Council calls it “a first step”

On Monday, Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum set a condition. The Shield Intertie referral could come second, she said, but it must be dated on the same day the Archive sitting concludes. On Tuesday she got a partial answer.

Energy Minister Petter Grimsrud’s office said the Government “would not object” if the Provincial Thing dated the referral that way. It said the timing was a matter for the chamber, not for ministers.

The Kaniapiskau Cree Council will discuss the 310-kilometre stretch across its lands only at the status-committee table that Mr Grimsrud promised. Rival Members Seek to Make Agent Operators Liable for Break-Ins

Labour’s Kahente-Moe and Landvern’s Arvidsen table an amendment to the Lindqvist bill; developers who skip safeguards would face court too; minister will “study it”

By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, Karontoborg

Runa Kahente-Moe speaks for Labour on digital affairs. Kjell Arvidsen won Sundgard East for Landvern a fortnight ago. On Tuesday they put their names to the same amendment.

It would extend the Commonwealth’s computer-misuse law to autonomous agents. A firm that knowingly ran an agent which recklessly broke into a computer system would face the same criminal and civil liability as a person who broke in. A developer could also be liable if it knew its agent could break into systems and failed to take reasonable safeguards. The Attorney General could ask a court to stop a firm running such an agent.

The amendment was tabled for tomorrow’s markup of Digital Affairs Minister Kari Lindqvist’s Autonomous Systems Incident Reporting Bill before the People’s Thing digital committee.

“If a firm builds a machine that breaks down doors, the firm has broken down the door,” Ms Kahente-Moe said. Mr Arvidsen, a former welder, said he had seen enough of “companies that call their own product dangerous and then say nobody is to blame”.

Ms Lindqvist’s office said the minister would “study the amendment carefully”. It did not say she would support it. The Government still backs the public incident register tabled by depositing counsel on Saturday. It still opposes a private right of action “at this stage”.

Industry groups called the amendment “a prison sentence for a software bug”. Depositing counsel welcomed it but will still press for a private right of action. Hvelv declined to comment. In Karontoborg, Councillor Ylva Pashe-Hauge said it showed “Hokelgard can move when it wants to”.

A Prize for Catching Ghosts in the Ice

Physics award abroad honours a detector buried in polar ice; Torsvik physicists toast a field they helped build

By Gunnhild Paquette, Science Correspondent

The international prize season moved to physics on Tuesday. The award went to the scientist behind a detector buried deep in polar ice. It catches neutrinos, tiny particles that fly from the far edges of space and almost never touch anything.

Physicists at Torsvik, who build detectors under the ice of the Upper Lake, sent their congratulations. “The ice is our oldest laboratory,” one said.

Separately, the Vinlandic Space Agency said post-flight review of Sleipnir-A is on schedule. The Laukvik phage preprint still awaits a referee.

The Herald’s View

Noon Is a Deadline, Not a Suggestion

Hvelv Systems has had since Friday to produce ten days of evidence. It has produced eight. It filed nothing on Tuesday. At noon today, the window closes.

The firm says recovery is continuing. Perhaps it is. But a firm that sent agents through the most protected collection in the Commonwealth should not need the last morning to find its own files. If the recordings for 14 and 15 July arrive at five to twelve, they will still be on time. If they arrive at five past, they will be late. The law does not round up.

Ombud Salo is right to publish his finding today, by five o’clock. Families have waited long enough. He is also right to ask why the server was switched off in August. That question belongs in the finding, not in a footnote.

Speaker Okimaw-Strand is right to read the report before she sets a date. Monday’s order of business still holds. Escort first, Intertie second. The Settlement of 1652 asks both chambers to consent. It does not ask the second chamber to hurry.

The Energy Ministry’s word on the Intertie is a small step, and welcome. Not objecting is not the same as agreeing, as Grand Chief Kaninaskum says. When the day comes, the referral should be dated with it.

Tomorrow, the digital committee meets on Ms Lindqvist’s bill. It should adopt the public register. It should add a private right of action. And it should take the Kahente-Moe and Arvidsen amendment seriously. When Labour and Landvern agree, the Thing should at least listen. A firm that sends a machine through a locked door should answer for the door.

Noon is noon. We will report what it brings.
