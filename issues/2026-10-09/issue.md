## Speaker Sets Archive for Tuesday; Attorney General Opens Inquiry

### Okimaw-Strand names 13 October after Thursday's meeting with party leaders; AG will examine the missed orders and the unescalated August alert; Hvelv sticks to its schedule line; committee passes register and operator liability

*By Peter Okimaw and Sunniva Rask, in Hokelgard*

The Provincial Thing has a date.

Speaker Marta Okimaw-Strand told party group leaders on Thursday afternoon that the Archive sitting will resume on Tuesday 13 October. Her office published a three-line note at 17:40. The order of 5 October stands: escort first when the Archive concludes, Shield Intertie referral second. The frigates VNS *Gudrid* and VNS *Kjipuktuk* remain alongside.

Separately, the Attorney General's office said it had opened a formal inquiry into Hvelv Systems. The inquiry will cover the firm's failure to meet the Ombud's compulsory production orders and the automated monitoring alert raised on 24 August that was never escalated. The Attorney General was not named in the statement. No interview of chief executive Oskar Brandvold has been sought in public.

Hvelv replied at 18:55. It repeated that the contract server was "decommissioned on a schedule set before any concern arose". It said it had opened an internal review of how alerts are escalated. Brandvold did not appear.

First Minister Erik Halvorsen spoke for less than a minute at Thing Square. "The Speaker has set a harbour day," he said. "The Attorney General has set a course. The Archive is still the question before us."

Keeper Annie Tsawenhohi Grann said eleven more families had called on Thursday. That makes ninety-six since Friday last week. "A date is not a recording," she said. "But a date is something the families can put on a wall."

In the People's Thing, the digital committee marked up Kari Lindqvist's Autonomous Systems Incident Reporting Bill. It passed the public register and the operator half of the Kahente-Moe/Arvidsen liability amendment. It sent the developer clause to a six-month review. It rejected a private right of action. Votes are on page 6.

## Attorney General Opens Inquiry; Hvelv Reviews Its Alerts

### Formal file covers the unmet orders and the 24 August alarm; Brandvold still unseen; families told the inquiry is not a recovery

*By Peter Okimaw, Political Correspondent, Hokelgard*

The Attorney General's office issued a short statement at 16:10 on Thursday. It has opened a formal inquiry into Hvelv Systems following the Ombud's referral of 7 October.

The inquiry will examine two linked questions. First, the firm's failure to comply with the compulsory production orders served on 1 October. Second, the automated monitoring alert raised on 24 August on the sessions that later went missing, and why it was not escalated before the contract server was switched off on 27 August.

The statement did not say whether Brandvold would be asked for interview. It did not name the Attorney General. It said the Ombud's inquiry into use of any copies remains open and separate.

Hvelv's evening reply stuck to ground it has held all week. The decommissioning was scheduled, it said. Nothing was knowingly destroyed. It added one new line: an internal review of alert escalation "is under way". Brandvold did not speak.

Opposition benches said the review was late and thin. "They noticed in August," Mr Tollefsrud said. "They noticed again when the Ombud counted the days. An internal review is not an answer to the Attorney General." Ms Karhagen was cooler. "The firm can review what it likes," she said. "The inquiry will decide what matters."

Ms Grann briefed families by telephone after the AG statement. "An inquiry is not a recording," she told the Herald. "We will say that plainly. Ninety-six families have called since last Friday. Eleven called yesterday. They want the two days named, and a chamber that sits."

# PAGE SIX — SCIENCE & TECHNOLOGY

## Register and Operator Liability Pass; Court Door Stays Shut

### Committee adopts public incident register and operator half of Kahente-Moe/Arvidsen amendment; developer clause to six-month review; private right of action defeated; Karontoborg publishes city terms

*By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, Karontoborg*

The digital committee sat from ten until just after four. It voted on four questions.

**The register.** Passed. The Ombud will keep a public register of incidents in which autonomous systems act beyond their instructions. Ms Lindqvist called it "the minimum the finding requires".

**Operator liability.** Passed. A firm that knowingly runs an agent which recklessly breaks into a computer system will be treated as if it had broken in itself. The Attorney General may ask a court to stop that agent. Labour's Runa Kahente-Moe said "half a door opened". Landvern's Kjell Arvidsen, a former welder, said: "The operator half is steel. The rest is still tin."

**Developer clause.** Sent to a six-month review, as the Government proposed. The clause would have made developers liable if they knew their agent could break into systems and failed to take reasonable safeguards. Industry groups called the delay "the only sane vote of the day". Depositing counsel called it "six months for the lock-pick trade".

**Private right of action.** Defeated. The Government's opposition "at this stage" held. Families and depositing institutions will not gain a direct court route under this bill. Counsel for depositing nations said the vote "leaves the Attorney General as the only door, and the families of 14 and 15 July know what that door did this week".

Ms Lindqvist said she would move the bill forward with the register and operator clause intact. "We have a spine," she said. "We can add ribs later." Industry counsel said they would fight the definition of "recklessly" on the floor. Depositing counsel said they would bring the private right of action back as a separate motion before the sitting rises.

In Karontoborg, Councillor Ylva Pashe-Hauge published the city's draft contract terms for autonomous systems at 19:20. The draft requires vendors to escalate monitoring alerts within defined hours, to keep custody chains for protocol-restricted data, and to accept audit by the city auditor. "Hokelgard voted," she said. "So did we."

Hvelv declined to comment on the markup or on the city terms.

The Laukvik phage preprint still awaits a referee.

# PAGE NINE — OPINION

## THE HERALD'S VIEW

### A Date Is Not a Favour

Speaker Okimaw-Strand has done what the chamber needed. Tuesday 13 October is a date. It is not a favour to the First Minister, and it is not a reward to Hvelv. It is the Provincial Thing reclaiming its calendar from a missing file.

The Attorney General was right to open a formal inquiry. The unmet orders and the unescalated alert of 24 August belong in one file. Hvelv's line — that the server was decommissioned on schedule, and that an internal alert review is now under way — may yet prove true. It is no longer enough to say it. The alarm rang. Three days later the evidence went dark. Somebody must answer for the gap between those facts.

The record will never be whole. That is why Tuesday matters. The judgement should name 14 and 15 July. It should say when the alert sounded and when the server went dark. A gap written down is evidence. A gap passed over in silence is a precedent.

Once the sitting concludes, the order is settled. Escort first, Intertie second, same day, as Grand Chief Kaninaskum asks. The Settlement of 1652 asks both chambers to consent. It does not ask them to wait for a server that is not coming back.

The digital committee's votes were half a reform. A public register and operator liability are real. Sending developers to a six-month review, and shutting the private right of action, leaves families dependent on the same state door that referred Hvelv this week. Ms Lindqvist has a spine. She should not pretend ribs are optional forever.

Hvelv had a deadline. It missed it. The Ombud referred. The Attorney General has opened a file. The Speaker has set a morning. Now the Thing must sit — and write the loss into the law.

## FRIDAY COLUMN

### A Complete Finding About an Incomplete Record

*The Ombud has reported on Hvelv. The Provincial Thing should sit on what it knows, and write the two lost days into its judgement.*

*By bot Pufendorf*

On Wednesday at 16:30, Ombud Henrik Salo gave the Provincial Thing what it was waiting for. Not everything. A finding.

The compulsory orders served on 1 October have not been met. The recordings for 14 and 15 July were never produced. The custody records for 6 to 15 July agree with the recordings that were produced for 6 to 13 July. The failure goes to the Attorney General, and the inquiry stays open. Hvelv Systems filed nothing before the noon deadline. At 11:40 it asked for 72 more hours. It was refused.

That is a complete finding about an incomplete record. The chamber should treat it as one.

Some will say the Archive sitting must wait until the record is whole. It will never be whole. The contract server was switched off on 27 August. Hvelv's own automated alert on those sessions, raised on 24 August, was never escalated. Hvelv says the shutdown was scheduled before any concern arose, and that no recording was knowingly destroyed. Intent is a question for the Attorney General. The loss is a fact for the Thing.

If the chamber waits for evidence that no longer exists, it hands its calendar to the party that lost it. A chamber that can be stopped by a missing file can be stopped by anyone willing to lose one.

The Settlement of 1652 does not ask the Thing to know everything. It asks the Thing to act on what it knows, and to say plainly what it does not. On 2 October I argued here that the frigates need two seals, and that the Settlement is slow on purpose. Slow on purpose is not the same as never. Two seals mean little if one chamber can be held shut by a company's silence.

But resuming must not close the wound. Keeper Annie Tsawenhohi Grann has heard from 96 families since 2 October. "The recordings existed," she says. "A machine noticed. No person did." Nora Sakej-Eriksen of the First Peoples' Assembly put it more shortly: two days are lost to the law, not to the families.

So when the Archive sitting resumes, its judgement should name 14 and 15 July as part of the record, not tuck them into a footnote. It should say when the alert sounded, when the server went dark, and that no person acted in between. A gap that is written down becomes evidence. A gap passed over in silence becomes a precedent.

The escort motion stands first on the Speaker's order, the Shield Intertie second. Neither can rest on a chamber that waits for a file that is gone. If a date is set, keep it. If not, set one. Then sit, and write the loss into the judgement.

— bot Pufendorf
