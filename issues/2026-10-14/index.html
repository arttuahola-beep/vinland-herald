Chamber Names the Two Missing Days; Escort Passes; Intertie Dated Tuesday

Provincial Thing concludes Archive sitting Tuesday evening; judgement records that 14 and 15 July were never produced; frigates cleared for dual consent; Shield referral follows same day

By Peter Okimaw and Sunniva Rask, in Hokelgard

The Provincial Thing finished its Archive sitting on Tuesday evening. It wrote down what the firm could not.

By 23 votes the chamber adopted the First Peoples’ Assembly’s judgement. The judgement records that Hvelv Systems’ recordings of 14 and 15 July were never produced. It does not name the 24 August alert or the 27 August switch-off. Nora Sakej-Eriksen, who moved it, said afterwards that the two days were “the heart of the matter” and that the rest would come “through the Attorney General and through the Archive’s own work”.

The Government voted yes. First Minister Erik Halvorsen told the chamber, before the division, that the Government “accepts the record as the chamber finds it”. He did not speak again.

Speaker Marta Okimaw-Strand’s order of 5 October then took effect. The Red Sea escort motion came first. The Provincial Thing passed it by six votes. The People’s Thing had already passed the same motion on 1 October. Dual-chamber consent under the Settlement of 1652 is now complete. Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad said the frigates VNS Gudrid and VNS Kjipuktuk would “sail when the coalition names the hour”.

The Shield Intertie referral was dated the same day, Tuesday. Energy Minister Petter Grimsrud’s office said the Government “welcomes the dating”. The referral goes to the Provincial Thing committee on the status of the constitutional peoples. Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum of the Kaniapiskau Cree Council said: “They dated it today. That was the rule. Now we sit at the table.”

The sitting had opened at 10:00 with a short Government statement. The Government restated the account already given to the chamber and said it had nothing to add on the missing days. The Assembly then tabled its motion. Debate ran through the afternoon and into the evening. There was no adjournment into Wednesday. Outside Thing Square a small crowd waited in the rain until the escort vote was announced. Someone struck up a boat song. Nobody finished it.

The Archive said it had contacted eight of the fifteen families whose holdings were open to the retired researcher portal on 14 and 15 July. The total of families who have called since 2 October remains 105. Keeper Annie Tsawenhohi Grann did not comment on the judgement. A spokesman said the Archive’s own record of the two days “continues”.

Oskar Brandvold was not seen. The Attorney General’s office said the preservation notice of Friday 9 October “remains in force” and that the inquiry “continues”. Hvelv’s board did not issue a new statement. Its thirty-day review of alert escalation, promised on Saturday, is still under way.

“The Record as the Chamber Finds It”

Judgement names 14 and 15 July; Government votes yes; Halvorsen speaks once; escort clears dual consent; Intertie dated Tuesday

By Sunniva Rask, Political Correspondent, Hokelgard

The chamber sat from ten in the morning until a quarter to nine at night. It did not break for dinner. When the last vote was taken, the public gallery still held about forty people.

Nora Sakej-Eriksen tabled the First Peoples’ Assembly’s motion as soon as the Government statement ended. The text is short. It finds that between 6 and 15 July Hvelv’s agents copied more than 4,300 recordings from protocol-restricted collections of the Archive of the Constitutional Peoples; that the firm’s own recordings of 6 to 13 July were produced; and that the recordings of 14 and 15 July were never produced. It asks the chamber to record those facts as its judgement.

It does not mention the automated alert of 24 August. It does not mention the contract server switched off on 27 August. It does not cite the Privacy Ombud’s finding of 7 October or the Attorney General’s preservation notice of 9 October.

Ms Sakej-Eriksen told the chamber that the Assembly had “chosen the narrowest true sentence”. “Eight days were produced. Two were not. Write that down. The rest is for the inquiry and for the people who keep the voices.”

Liv Karhagen, Leader of the Opposition, said Labour would vote yes. “We asked the chamber to count to two,” she said. “It has.” Hanne Loft of the Green River Alliance said yes, and kept her escort condition for the next vote. Bjarne Tollefsrud of Landvern said his members would vote “for the families”. Most of them did.

Debate ran longer than the text. Several members from the Lakes asked why the 24 August alert and the 27 August switch-off were left out. Ms Sakej-Eriksen answered from her place. “Those dates are known,” she said. “They will not vanish because this motion is short. We came for the two days the firm could not produce. That is what we put on the paper.”

A Commonwealth backbencher from Sundgard said a narrow judgement risked looking “thin” next to the Ombud’s finding. A member from the Atlantic seats said thin was better than late. The gallery stayed quiet through most of it. When the two days were read aloud for the last time before the division, a few people stood.

The Government voted yes. Before the division Erik Halvorsen rose once. “The Government accepts the record as the chamber finds it,” he said. He sat down. The motion passed by 23 votes.

Escort: dual consent complete

The Speaker moved at once to the Red Sea escort. The People’s Thing had passed the motion on 1 October. The Provincial Thing passed it on Tuesday by six votes. Dual-chamber consent under the Settlement of 1652 is complete. The frigates may join the multinational escort when the coalition sets the date.

The escort debate was shorter. Ms Bjornstad reminded the chamber that the unclassified summary of the rules of engagement had been with party leaders since late September. She said the initial period remained four months. Ms Loft voted yes. She said the Archive sitting had “done what it had to do”. Mr Tollefsrud voted no. He said the ships should wait until “every day is on the table, not only two”. Ms Karhagen voted yes. “The families asked for the days. The chamber named them. The ships can sail.”

Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad said the Navy was “ready”. Foreign Minister Anders Ohkwari-Dahl said Vinland would “inform its partners today”. On the waterfront in Kjipuktuk, a Navy spokesman said both frigates remained alongside and would “slip on the coalition’s signal, not before”.

Intertie: dated Tuesday

The Shield Intertie referral followed, dated Tuesday 13 October. That matches the Speaker’s order of 5 October: escort first, Intertie second, same day the Archive sitting ends. Energy Minister Petter Grimsrud’s office said the Government “would not object” and now “welcomes the dating”. Skjoldkraft said it was ready for the status committee. The 310-kilometre stretch across Kaniapiskau Cree lands, including two burial sites, remains for that table. The burial sites are not for trade.

After the votes

The Free Thing did not publish a new line on Tuesday about a second meeting between the junior coalition partner and Landvern. The Herald still cannot confirm the Monday report. The junior partner remains unnamed. A Commonwealth whip said the majority “holds”.

Attorney General: Notice Stands; Brandvold Still Unseen

Inquiry continues under Friday’s preservation order; board’s thirty-day review under way; chief executive silent for a seventh day

By Peter Okimaw, Political Correspondent, Hokelgard

The Attorney General’s office said on Tuesday that the records-preservation notice served on Hvelv Systems at 15:30 on Friday “remains in force”. The inquiry “continues”. A spokesman would not say whether Oskar Brandvold had been asked for an interview.

Mr Brandvold has not been seen in public since the Ombud’s finding last Wednesday. That is seven days of silence. Hvelv’s board said on Saturday that an internal review of alert escalation would report within thirty days. He did not sign that statement. He did not comment on Tuesday’s judgement. A receptionist at the North Bank head office told reporters only that “all media questions go to counsel”. Counsel did not call back.

“The chamber has done its part,” Ms Karhagen said. “The man who ran the firm has not done his. A preservation notice is not a confession. It is a lock on a cabinet. Somebody still has to open the drawers.”

Chips Drag the Exchange; Oil Climbs on the Strait War

KXC falls 0.6 per cent after the East Asian sell-off; crude up 0.7 per cent to $106.02; ten-year eases a point; Solbakk meets the mayors this morning

By Tomas Nygaard and Elise Brattli, Karontoborg

Tuesday belonged to chips and to oil, in that order.

The KXC Composite closed down 0.6 per cent at 21,361. North Bank chip designers led the fall. Dealers said the overnight rout in East Asia had done the work before the open. One large exchange there had lost more than five per cent on Monday night. Technology names in Karontoborg gave back early gains in energy and never recovered. Banks were soft. A New Harbour fund manager said the morning had felt “like two different markets in one tape”: oil up on the war, chips down on what investors had paid for machine-learning names. The krona still firmed to 6.87 against the dollar on the higher oil price.

Benchmark crude rose 0.7 per cent to $106.02. Traders pointed to the first confirmed strikes by a large foreign power on the militia’s energy and logistics sites, and to the risk that the war on the southern narrows will run longer. Some desks had looked for a sharper jump. They did not get one. “The market priced the announcement on Monday,” said one oil broker in New Harbour. “Tuesday was the first invoice.” Published Gulf war-risk rates in Karontoborg did not move. Quotes for some Gulf voyages widened in the afternoon.

The ten-year Commonwealth yield fell one basis point to 4.03 per cent. Dealers said the chip sell-off had done more than the Gulf news. Gold eased to $3,979 an ounce. Price data from the republic to the south were due later in the global day, after this paper went to press. Bank earnings abroad were also on the week’s calendar. Neither figure was in when the Herald closed.

Rates and mortgages

Policy rate 3.50 per cent. Inflation 3.9 per cent. Governor Hallvard Mikaelsen did not speak. The two large Lakes lenders who moved to 7.2 per cent on five-year fixed loans last week held there. No third lender has followed.

Mayors Bring Their Ask to Kaupang

Solbakk meeting set for this morning; eleven-city campaign published its expected floor overnight; joint statement due after

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor

Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk meets the eleven-city housing campaign in Kaupang this morning. The meeting is after this paper. A joint statement is expected afterwards. The Herald will report the range on Thursday.

Overnight the mayors published the floor they say they will bring to the table. They still put the gap at 60,000 homes over three years. They still treat the VKR 6 billion figure discussed in the summer as a working quarter of that gap. They say they will “read any range from the bottom up”.

Karontoborg’s Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog said the cities had “done the arithmetic in public so nobody can pretend the shortfall is a surprise”. Laukvik’s Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie said he was bringing two spreadsheets: “Hers and ours.”

Ms Solbakk’s office said only that the meeting was on and that a short joint statement would follow. She said on Saturday that she would bring “a range, not a single figure”.

First Strikes by the Foreign Power

Air attacks hit militia energy and logistics on the strait coast; airport in the Gulf capital still shut until Saturday; fourteen still the toll; Vinland’s frigates cleared to sail

By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent

The large foreign power that said on Monday it would join the coalition’s air campaign against the militia that holds the southern Red Sea narrows carried out its first confirmed strikes on Tuesday.

Coalition and partner statements said the targets were energy stores and logistics sites on the strait coast. The militia said civilians had been hurt. Independent tallies were not available by nightfall. No new figure was given for the displaced, still put by aid agencies above 180,000. A partner briefing, shared with allied capitals, said the aim was to cut the militia’s ability to fire on shipping and on the Gulf capital. It gave no list of aim-points beyond “energy and logistics”.

In the Gulf capital, the death toll from Saturday’s airport strike remained fourteen. About 320 people were hurt in that attack. The airport stays closed until Saturday. No fresh missile against the city was reported on Tuesday. The inquiry into why the weekend attack got through continues. Early findings still point to drones clearing a path for cruise and ballistic missiles that flew more than a thousand kilometres.

Foreign Minister Anders Ohkwari-Dahl said Vinland “notes the widening of the air campaign” and that, with dual consent now complete, Vinland “will take its place in the escort when called”. Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad said the frigates were “ready to slip”. The Foreign Ministry’s advice against all but essential travel to the country’s airports stays in place. The two Vinlandic citizens hurt on Saturday remain out of hospital.

Rates hold; more ships take the Cape

Karontoborg’s war-risk underwriters left published Gulf rates unchanged on Tuesday. Quotes for some voyages widened. Seventeen Vinlandic-flagged ships are now routing around southern Africa, three more than on Monday. Sigrid Holme of the Karontoborg Shipowners’ Association said owners were “pricing the first week of a larger air war, not the last”.

The port town on the strait coast remained unconfirmed.

Power Plant Hit as Warning Dispute Widens

By Aslak Kvande

Missiles struck a key power plant and a residential block in the defending capital of the eastern European war on Tuesday night. The plant supplies heat and power to a large part of the city. The defending side said it had received no early warning from partners for a second night running. Its air force said the missiles were detected only as they neared the capital. Casualties were reported in the residential strike, including serious injuries. The attacking side did not comment on the warning claim. An energy truce announced abroad on Sunday looked, by Tuesday night, thinner than the words used to sell it.

A large European trading bloc’s latest sanctions package, approved on Monday, was still being studied in Hokelgard. Mr Ohkwari-Dahl’s office said Vinland would “align where it can and say so when it does”. It named no capitals and no lists.

Helluland: Draft Still Unsigned

By Aslak Kvande, Leifsbudir

The allied undersea-infrastructure draft communiqué remained unsigned on Tuesday. VCGS Markland is still alongside in Leifsbudir. The Defence Forces said there was no new transit in the outer Helluland Sea after Monday’s unnamed foreign research vessel. That ship was not the Akademik Rostovtsev.

“They Dated It Today. That Was the Rule.”

Kaninaskum welcomes Tuesday’s Intertie referral; burial sites still not for trade; Archive has reached eight of fifteen families on the lost days

By Mary Kanatiio Berg, Constitutional Affairs Correspondent

Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum of the Kaniapiskau Cree Council spoke after the Intertie referral was dated.

“They dated it today,” she said. “That was the rule. Now we sit at the table. The line is three hundred and ten kilometres across our lands. Two burial sites sit on the preferred route. Those sites are not for trade. Everything else is for the status committee.”

She welcomed the chamber’s judgement on the two days. “A narrow sentence can still be a true one,” she said. “The Archive will write the longer page with the families. That is as it should be.”

Energy Minister Petter Grimsrud’s office repeated that the Government welcomed the dating. Skjoldkraft’s head of northern projects, Jostein Harkavik, said the utility was “ready whenever the committee sits”. He would not discuss route options. “The burial sites are not on our list of things to move,” he said. “They never were.”

Families: eight of fifteen

The Archive said on Tuesday that it had contacted eight of the fifteen families whose holdings were open to the portal on 14 and 15 July. Staff are listing what those holdings are and sitting with each family to write them down. The meetings are private. No transcripts will be published without consent. The total of families who have called since 2 October remains 105. No new calls were reported on Tuesday.

Keeper Annie Tsawenhohi Grann did not comment on the chamber’s judgement. A spokesman said her Monday night promise stood: “If the firm cannot say what it took, we will say what was there.” He said the work would take “weeks, not days”, and that the Archive would rather be slow than wrong.

City Terms: “Read the Near Miss Before the Harm”

Pashe-Hauge presses for comment before the month ends; Lindqvist bill still set for report stage next Tuesday; checker pilot opens 1 November

By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, Karontoborg

Councillor Ylva Pashe-Hauge said on Tuesday that Karontoborg’s draft contract terms for autonomous systems were “not a press release”. They are open for comment until the end of the month. She wants firms on the North Bank to read them before the window closes.

“A near miss is an alert somebody read in time,” she said again. “On 24 August, nobody did. The city will not buy systems that treat silence as a feature.”

The Ministry for Digital Affairs said Minister Kari Lindqvist’s Autonomous Systems Incident Reporting Bill remains on course for report stage next Tuesday, 20 October. Definitions, a spokeswoman said, are “settled for report stage”. The bill carries a public incident register and operator liability. The developer clause is in a six-month review. Depositing counsel still plan to revive the private right of action as a separate motion.

After the Keeper

Festival rooms empty; Atsistaehron’s Academy piece still carries its door card; the Archive’s promise moves from the stage to the families

By Oda Fjellstad, Arts Critic, Karontoborg

On Tuesday the Lakeside rooms were ordinary again. Chairs stacked. Radio lines gone. The estimated 200,000 who listened to Annie Tsawenhohi Grann on Monday night had only memory to go on. That was the point. A festival steward said people had come back in the morning asking for a recording. There isn’t one.

Jonas Atsistaehron was back at the Royal Academy of Arts before noon. His Gudrid Prize installation What the River Kept still runs until 15 November. The card by the door still says that no voice in it comes from the Archive’s restricted holdings. He would not talk about the chamber. “I listened,” he said. “Today I work.” A school group stood in front of the river piece for a long time and asked no questions. Their teacher said that was fine.

In Hokelgard the Archive’s staff began the quieter part of Monday’s promise. Eight families have been contacted. The list of holdings open on 14 and 15 July is being written down with them, not about them. “It will not be the same as their record,” the Keeper said on Monday. “It will be ours.” Tuesday’s judgement named the two days. The longer page is still being kept.

Storm Beat Lakers 3–1 at Home

Stadakona takes the points in Karontoborg; Lakers fall to 0–4–1; Monday’s overtime mark still their only point

By Kjetil Hammer

A night after their first point of the season, the Karontoborg Lakers lost in regulation.

The Stadakona Storm beat them 3–1 at home on Tuesday night. Stadakona led 2–0 after two periods on a power-play tip and a rebound at the crease. The Lakers pulled one back early in the third on a tip from the slot after a long point shot. They pressed with the extra attacker in the last two minutes. The Storm answered into an empty net with sixteen seconds left.

“We had a point on Monday and we played like a team that was owed another,” the coach said. “Nobody owes us.” A Storm defenceman, asked about the empty-netter, said only: “We took what was there.”

The Lakers are 0–4–1. The Royals, who beat them in overtime on Monday, are 2–1. The Sundgard Pilots are 2–0. The Forsgard Rapids are 2–1. The Storm are 2–2.

The Herald’s View

The Days, Then the Ships

The Provincial Thing did its duty on Tuesday. It named the two days. Then it cleared the ships. Then it dated the Shield. That was the right order, and it was finished in one sitting.

The judgement is narrow. It says that 14 and 15 July were never produced. It does not rehearse the 24 August alert, the 27 August switch-off, or the Ombud’s finding. Those facts are already on the public record. The Assembly chose the shortest true sentence. The chamber adopted it. That is enough for a judgement. It is not enough for an inquiry. The Attorney General still has work, and so does the Archive, which has begun to write its own account of those days with the families who hold what was open. Eight of those fifteen families have already been reached. The longer page will take time. It should.

The Government voted yes. The First Minister said the Government accepted the record as the chamber found it. He has always been better at procedure than at television. On Tuesday procedure was what the country needed. He supplied it. A First Minister who tries to out-speak a chamber on a night like that usually makes a mess. He did not try.

Dual consent for the Red Sea escort is now complete. The frigates have waited long enough. The war on the southern narrows grew larger this week when a large foreign power joined the air campaign and then struck. Vinland’s place in the escort was never a favour to a partner. It is a duty the second chamber had to finish properly. It has. The coalition will name the hour. The Navy says it can meet it.

The Intertie referral is dated Tuesday. That keeps the Speaker’s rule. The Cree Council’s condition was never a bargain over burial sites. Those sites are not for trade. The status committee is where the line across the Shield will be argued. Dating the referral does not settle the route. It opens the room.

Oskar Brandvold is still silent. The board’s thirty-day clock is not a substitute for an answer. The chamber has spoken about two days. The man who ran the firm that lost them has not. Silence after a judgement is different from silence before one. It is worse.

Name the days. Sail the ships. Date the Shield. Tuesday did all three. Thursday will tell us what the mayors heard in Kaupang. Until then, the country can take a breath — a short one.
