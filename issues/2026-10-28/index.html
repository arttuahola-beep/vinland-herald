Fourteen Billion Launched. The Terms Have No Date

Solbakk and the Lakes government open the industry package in Karontoborg; conditions still “firm by firm”, with no timetable; the road north will begin with a route study put out to tender

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor, and Tomas Nygaard, Karontoborg

The industry package was launched on Tuesday. Its rules were not.

Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk and the Lakes provincial government announced the VKR 14 billion package in Karontoborg. The figures were the ones officials gave on Monday night: VKR 8 billion for steel, VKR 4 billion for vehicle lines and VKR 2 billion for a road to the mineral belt of the northern Shield.

The conditions were also as before. They will be set “firm by firm, in the contracts”. Asked when the public would see them, Ms Solbakk did not give a date.

“The terms will be right before they are quick,” she said.

First Minister Erik Halvorsen stood beside her. “We have set the course,” he said. “The charts come next.”

Labour will force a debate on the conditions. A Laukvik steelmaker said it would pause a line from December. And the road north will begin with a route study, put out to tender.

A Long Day in the Second Chamber

Status committee sits at ten in closed session on the Cree record; Incident Reporting Bill gets its first reading in the afternoon; Labour to force a debate on the package’s terms

By Sunniva Rask, Hokelgard

The Provincial Thing has two pieces of business today. Both have taken weeks to reach this point.

At ten o’clock the status committee meets in private to hear the Kaniapiskau Cree Council’s record of trapping-ground holders. Staff have set the whole morning aside. Page 5.

In the afternoon the chamber gives the Incident Reporting Bill its first reading. The Speaker’s office said on Tuesday that the order paper had not changed.

The first reading is a formality. The real work begins in committee in November.

The First Peoples’ Assembly, which holds the balance in the chamber, has still not said how it will treat the bill. Landvern said nothing new on Tuesday about how it would vote on a dated amendment. Its leader, Bjarne Tollefsrud, said on Monday: “Show us the date first.”

Minister Kari Lindqvist’s office repeated that the public register will open “in the spring”.

Labour wants the terms on the floor

Labour will use its next opposition day to force a debate on the conditions of the industry package, its finance spokesman, Oddvar Ruud, said on Tuesday.

“The Government has announced fourteen billion and no rules,” Mr Ruud said. “We will give it a whole afternoon to explain why. If the rules are ready, it can read them out. If they are not, it can tell us when.”

Labour did not say when its next opposition day would fall.

Liv Karhagen, the Leader of the Opposition, put it more simply. “Workers, not shareholders. Those are the First Minister’s words. We only want them in ink.”

“Late, and Karontoborg first”

Mr Tollefsrud was less kind about the package.

“It is late, and it is Karontoborg first,” he said in Hokelgard. “The men in Sundgard have been waiting since the summer. Now they will wait for contracts. And the money will go to whoever has the best lawyers on the North Bank.”

A Finance Ministry spokesman said any firm in the Lakes could apply, “wherever its head office is”.

Review continues

The Attorney General’s office said on Tuesday that its review of the Hvelv board minutes was “well advanced”. It gave no date. It has said the work will take “weeks, not days”. The preservation notice served on 9 October remains in force.

A Bill on Asking in Advance

Health Minister tables bill to let people with an illness that will take away their judgement ask in advance for an assisted death; mental illness alone would stay outside the law; provinces would decide whether to offer it

By Sunniva Rask

People with an illness that will slowly take away their judgement could ask in advance for an assisted death, under a bill tabled in the People’s Thing on Tuesday.

Health Minister Gunnar Wapistan said the bill would let a person write down, while they can still decide, the conditions under which they would want help to die later. The request would be checked by two doctors. It could be withdrawn at any time.

“Nothing in a request happens by itself,” Mr Wapistan said. “Every safeguard in the law still applies on the day. The person must still be suffering, and the doctors must still agree.”

The bill would also keep people whose only condition is a mental illness outside the law for good. Under the present law they would have become eligible on 1 April next year. The Government must therefore pass the bill through both chambers before then.

The provinces run most health care. Each province would decide whether to offer advance requests at all.

The Labour Federation said it would support the bill at first reading and study it in committee. Its health spokeswoman said the April date left “too little time for a law about life and death”.

A spokesman for the bishops of the Vinlandic Church said they would “read the bill slowly and say something carefully”. He would not say more.

A dementia families’ group welcomed the bill. A mental-health charity said it was “disappointed, though not surprised”.

The Launch: Same Numbers, Same Silence on Terms

Package opened in Karontoborg; applications “before the new year”; conditions with no timetable; Halvorsen: “The charts come next”; Labour to force a debate

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor

Tuesday’s announcement confirmed the size of the package. It did not add much else.

Ms Solbakk and the Lakes government’s industry minister presented it in Karontoborg. The Commonwealth will pay VKR 8.9 billion and the Lakes government VKR 5.1 billion.

The steel fund runs for five years, the vehicle money for two and the road for three. A joint steel task force will look first at the cost of moving steel.

Firms will be able to apply “before the new year”, Ms Solbakk said. Each firm will then negotiate its own contract.

No date for the rules

Asked three times when the conditions would be published, she gave the same answer.

“The terms will be right before they are quick,” she said. “A steel mill and a parts supplier are different firms. They will have different contracts.”

Would firms be asked to keep jobs? “Jobs are the point of the package,” she said. Would they be allowed to pay dividends while they held public money? “That will be in each contract.”

Mr Ruud was not satisfied. Of Mr Halvorsen’s line about charts, he said: “A course with no charts is how ships end up on rocks,” he said. “The First Minister should know that better than anyone.”

Who will apply

Two steelmakers said on Tuesday that they would apply: Sørstrand Steel in Laukvik and the main mill in Sundgard. The Sundgard vehicle works did not say. Its owners said they were “studying the details”.

The spokesman for the Lakes steel producers said the industry needed the money “this winter, not next”. “We are glad of the bridge,” he said. “We would like to know when we can walk on it.”

Sørstrand to Pause a Line in Laukvik

About 380 workers to move to short time from 1 December; company blames the cost of moving steel and cheap imports; mayor: “This is what waiting costs”

By Elise Brattli

Sørstrand Steel will pause one of its two strip lines in Laukvik from 1 December.

About 380 workers will move to short time, the company said on Tuesday. It said no one would lose their job “for now”. It did not say how long the pause would last.

The company gave two reasons. The cost of moving steel by ship has risen sharply since the Gulf crisis began. And cheap imports, it said, have taken orders the mill used to win. It did not name the countries.

Sørstrand said it would apply to the new steel fund as soon as applications open.

Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie of Laukvik said the news showed why the conditions mattered.

“This is what waiting costs,” he said. “Three hundred and eighty families will have a poorer Christmas while the contracts are drafted. I want the money to arrive. And I want it to keep these people at work when it does.”

Kjell Arvidsen, the Landvern member for Sundgard East, said Laukvik was “a warning for Sundgard”. “Every week without a contract is a week a line can stop,” he said.

Land-Test Answer Promised for Thursday

Treasury says it will tell the working group whether federal land counts toward the cities’ land test

By Elise Brattli

Treasury will answer the land-test question on Thursday, it said on Tuesday.

Labour asked eight days ago whether federal land handed to the cities would count toward the land test in the housing compact. Treasury has not said. On Tuesday a spokesman said the answer would be given “at the table” when the housing working group meets.

The group will discuss terms for thirty-seven of the fifty sites on the federal list. The other thirteen are claimed by nations and will go to a separate table, which first meets on Friday 13 November.

“Thursday, then,” said Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog of Karontoborg. “We will bring a pen.”

The fifty sites cover 613 hectares.

Miners Lead; Crude Slips

KXC up 0.6 per cent to 21,250 on copper; crude down 1.1 per cent to $117.73 despite tanker fire; ten-year yield falls three points

By Tomas Nygaard, Karontoborg

Mining shares led the Karontoborg market on Tuesday.

The KXC Composite rose 0.6 per cent to 21,250. Copper and nickel producers gained most, as the copper price rose sharply abroad. Chip designers also rose. Energy producers fell with oil.

Benchmark crude fell 1.1 per cent to $117.73 a barrel. It was the first fall in three sessions. Traders pointed to Monday’s talks in the north-shore power’s capital, and set those against the news of mines near the strait. “Hope beat fear today,” one trader said. “Only just.”

The ten-year Commonwealth yield fell three basis points to 3.93 per cent. The krona was unchanged at 6.85 to the dollar and 7.92 to the euro. Gold rose $19 to $4,030 an ounce.

Lakes lenders kept their five-year fixed rate at 7.2 per cent. The Central Bank said nothing new. Most economists now expect it to raise its policy rate at its next meeting. The rate is 3.50 per cent. Inflation is 3.9 per cent.

Mines East of the Strait

North-shore power says a tanker struck mines and caught fire while trying to cross a “restricted” area; it calls the strait “closed” to ships that do not coordinate with its forces; hundreds of ships wait

By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent

An oil tanker struck mines east of the strait on Tuesday and caught fire, the navy of the north-shore power said.

The navy said the ship had switched off its tracking system and tried to cross a “restricted and unsafe” area. It did not name the tanker or its flag. It did not say whether the crew were safe, or who had laid the mines.

The strait, the navy said, remains “closed” to any ship that does not coordinate its passage with the country’s armed forces.

Shipping agents in the region said hundreds of ships were now waiting at the approaches, some for permission and some for the danger to pass.

No Vinlandic ship was involved, the Karontoborg Shipowners’ Association said.

“A minefield with a permission desk,” said Sigrid Holme of the association. “That is not a shipping lane. That is a toll gate with explosives.”

It was the third ship reported struck or burning since Friday.

Rates up a step

Karontoborg’s war-risk underwriters raised their published Gulf rates by one step on Tuesday. It is the second rise since Friday.

The number of Vinlandic-flagged ships on the long route round the Cape was unchanged at twenty-five.

Frigates in the region

VNS Gudrid and VNS Kjipuktuk have arrived in the region, the Navy said on Tuesday.

“Both ships are on station and ready,” said Commander Einar Paul, the Navy spokesman. He said escorts had not yet begun. The Navy would not say when the first would take place, or where.

The initial escort period is four months.

The Foreign Ministry had no comment on the mines.

By Aslak Kvande, Leifsbudir

The Families’ Record, Behind Closed Doors

Status committee hears the Cree Council’s record of trapping-ground holders this morning; nothing new on who pays; finding not expected today

By Mary Kanatiio Berg, Constitutional Affairs Correspondent

At ten o’clock this morning the doors of a committee room in the Thinghall will close.

Inside, the Kaniapiskau Cree Council will show the Provincial Thing’s status committee its record of trapping-ground holders. The record names the families who hold and use each trapping ground on the Shield. Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum has said it will not be made public.

The committee asked last Thursday who “the families” are. It wanted to know who speaks for a family, and how consent is given and written down. The Council has said it will answer both questions today.

The Council’s condition is that no line may cross a named trapping ground without the consent of the families who hold it.

Ms Kaninaskum had nothing more to say on Tuesday. She said on Monday that the Council would “return the courtesy” of the chair, who granted the private session.

The private session on the burial-site maps was due to last an hour. It lasted more than two. The whole morning has been set aside today. A finding is not expected.

Still not decided

Energy Minister Petter Grimsrud said nothing new on Tuesday about who will pay the extra VKR 1.1 billion for Skjoldkraft’s second route. That route is 21 km longer and keeps wider of both burial sites. He has said the cost is “not decided”.

The Road Will Start With a Study

Lakes government to put a route study to tender before a route is chosen; First Peoples’ Assembly tables a written question: will the nations help write the study’s terms?

By Mary Kanatiio Berg

The road to the northern mineral belt will begin on paper.

The Lakes government’s industry minister said on Tuesday that a study of possible routes would be put out to tender first. Only then would a route be chosen.

“We will not draw a line on a map in an office,” the minister said. “We will study the ground first.”

The minister did not say who would write the study’s terms. That is the question the First Peoples’ Assembly asked on Tuesday.

The Assembly tabled a written question in the Provincial Thing. It asks whether the nations whose ground the road may cross will help to set the terms of the study, and whether they will see it before a route is chosen.

“A study can ask the right questions or the wrong ones,” said Nora Sakej-Eriksen, the Assembly’s leader. “It depends who writes them. We asked on Monday who had been consulted. On Tuesday we were told there will be a tender. A tender is not a conversation.”

“We asked in writing because we want the answer in writing,” she said.

A Finance Ministry spokesman said the study would “include the usual consultation”.

A Harbour, Three Kitchens and a Long Silence

Brita Solem’s Salt Road follows one Kjipuktuk family through three kitchens and two generations; it is her quietest book, and her best

By Oda Fjellstad, Arts Critic

Brita Solem chaired this year’s Gudrid Prize jury. Now her own work is in the shops.

Salt Road is her sixth novel. It is set in Kjipuktuk, in three kitchens: a grandmother’s, a mother’s and a daughter’s. Each kitchen is a part of the book. Each part covers a single winter.

Not much happens. A boat is sold. A man does not come home from a fishing season, and nobody says why. A daughter moves to Karontoborg and comes back. The grandmother speaks Mi’kmaq to the cat and Vinlandic to everyone else.

What happens is in the silences. Ms Solem is very good at what families do not say. One of the best scenes is a quarrel about a recipe for salt cod. It is really about a debt nobody has mentioned for twenty years.

The prose is plain, which suits the book. Kjipuktuk in winter is not a place for long speeches.

The book has faults. The middle part drags. The daughter’s years in Karontoborg are told, not shown, and the city never feels as real as the harbour.

But the last section is very fine. The daughter, now a grandmother herself, sits in the first kitchen. She finally understands what her own grandmother kept from her, and why. Ms Solem does not explain it. She trusts the reader. It is a rare kind of trust.

Salt Road is her quietest book. It may be her best.

The Herald’s View

Write the Terms Before the Tender

On Tuesday the Commonwealth and the Lakes government launched their industry package. We had asked for three things: the figure, the firms and the conditions. We now have the figure, two firms, and a promise that the conditions will be “right before they are quick”.

That is not good enough, and it is not necessary.

The Finance Minister says each firm is different, so each contract must be different. Some of that is true. A steel mill and a parts supplier need different help. But some rules should be the same for every firm that takes public money. Will it keep its workers while it holds the money? Will it pay dividends to its shareholders in the meantime? These are not technical questions. They are the questions that decide whether the First Minister’s own words, “workers, not shareholders”, mean anything.

Those rules can be written in a week. They should be published before the first application arrives, which Ms Solbakk says will be “before the new year”. If the Government will not publish them, Labour’s planned debate is the right way to make it explain why.

Tuesday also showed why speed matters. Sørstrand Steel will pause a line in Laukvik on 1 December, and about 380 workers will go on short time. The company says it will apply to the fund. Good. But that is exactly the case that needs clear terms. If the line comes back with public money, the public should know what it has bought. We do not want the rules to delay the money. We want the rules and the money to arrive together.

Mr Halvorsen likes to speak of the sea. “We have set the course,” he said on Tuesday. “The charts come next.” With respect, a captain draws the charts before he leaves harbour.

The road north is the same lesson in a different place. The Lakes government says it will start with a study, put out to tender, before it chooses a route. That is sensible. But a study is only as good as its questions. The First Peoples’ Assembly asks whether the nations whose ground the road may cross will help to write them. That deserves a plain yes.

This morning the status committee sits behind closed doors with the record of the Cree families who hold the Intertie’s trapping grounds. It is there because a line was drawn first and the families were asked later. The Government has the chance to do it the other way round this time. It should write the terms of the study with the nations, and put them in the tender. That costs nothing now. It could save years later.

Finally, a word for the Health Minister. Mr Wapistan has tabled a serious bill on a grave subject, and set out its safeguards clearly. Parliament has until April. It should use the time well, and not rush a law about life and death to meet a date.
