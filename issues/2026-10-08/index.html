Hvelv Misses Deadline; Ombud Sends Firm to Attorney General

Last two days of recordings never arrive; finding says the server was switched off three days after the firm’s own alarm flagged the sessions; Speaker will meet party leaders today but sets no date

By Peter Okimaw and Halldor Vik, in Hokelgard

The deadline passed at noon. The recordings did not arrive.

At 16:30 on Wednesday, Privacy Ombud Henrik Salo published his finding. The compulsory production orders served on Hvelv Systems on 1 October “have not been met”. He has referred the firm’s failure to the Attorney General.

The session recordings for 14 and 15 July, the last two days of the breach, were never filed. At 11:40, twenty minutes before the window closed, Hvelv asked for three more days. The Ombud’s office said no.

The finding also answered a question Hvelv would not. The contract server holding those two days was switched off on 27 August. Three days earlier, on 24 August, Hvelv’s own monitoring system had raised an automated alert on the same sessions. Nobody passed the alert on.

Hvelv says it identified the breach on 2 September. The Ombud did not dispute that date. But on his account, the firm’s systems noticed before the firm did.

Hvelv said in a statement at 19:10 that the server was “decommissioned on a schedule set before any concern arose”. It said no recording had been “knowingly destroyed” and that it would cooperate fully with the Attorney General. Chief executive Oskar Brandvold did not appear.

First Minister Erik Halvorsen spoke briefly at Thing Square. “Hvelv had a deadline and a chart. It missed both,” he said. “What happens to the firm is now a question for the Attorney General. The Archive is the question before us.”

The office of Speaker Marta Okimaw-Strand said she had received the finding and would read it in full. She will meet party leaders in the Provincial Thing today. No date has been set for the Archive sitting to resume.

Twenty Minutes to Noon, and a Request for Three More Days

How the window closed: a late plea, a firm refusal, a short finding; families of 14 and 15 July told the law will not bring their recordings back

By Peter Okimaw, Political Correspondent, Hokelgard

Hvelv’s last message to the Ombud came at 11:40 on Wednesday. Recovery from the switched-off server had “not been completed”, the firm wrote. It asked for seventy-two more hours.

The Ombud’s office replied before noon. The window had been set on 2 October, it said, and extended once already. It would not be extended again.

At noon the window closed. Staff spent the afternoon on a last check of the custody records, which cover all ten days from 6 to 15 July. The finding says they are consistent with the recordings Hvelv did produce, for 6 to 13 July.

The finding is about completeness, not use. It does not say what the copies were used for, or by whom. The inquiry stays open.

Annie Tsawenhohi Grann, Keeper of the Archive of the Constitutional Peoples, said eight more families had called on Wednesday. That makes eighty-five since Friday. “Fewer called,” she said. “I think they were waiting for five o’clock, as we were.”

“Now we know three things,” she said. “The recordings existed. A machine noticed. No person did.”

For the families of 14 and 15 July, the message is harder. “We will tell them the truth,” Ms Grann said. “The law has done what it can. It has not brought their recordings home.”

Nora Sakej-Eriksen, leader of the First Peoples’ Assembly, said: “Two days are lost to the law. They are not lost to the families.”

KXC down 0.7 per cent; Lakeshore Homes loses 2.6 per cent; crude eases as European partners speed up diesel release; ten-year yield dips

The ten-year Commonwealth yield fell two basis points to 4.07 per cent. Strong demand at a large bond sale abroad pulled yields off their highs. The krona weakened to 6.86 against a firmer dollar. Gold fell to $3,927 an ounce.

Abroad, a large Asian central bank raised its main rate for the first time in nearly four years. Minutes from another showed most of its officials expect one more rise before the year ends.

Mortgage Rates Climb Past 7 Per Cent; Solbakk Agrees to Meet the Mayors

Lenders lift five-year fixed quotes to 7.2 per cent; Finance Minister sets a Kaupang meeting for next Wednesday

By Tomas Nygaard, Karontoborg

Two of the largest Lakes lenders raised five-year fixed mortgage quotes to 7.2 per cent on Wednesday. Both blamed the cost of their own borrowing over the past fortnight.

Within hours, Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk’s office announced a meeting. She will see the eleven-city housing campaign in Kaupang on Wednesday 14 October. Her office said she would bring “the Ministry’s own arithmetic”.

On Tuesday the mayors published theirs. They said the cities had planned 180,000 housing starts over three years and were on course for about 120,000. That leaves 60,000 homes short. They said Ms Solbakk’s working figure of up to VKR 6 billion in borrowing relief would cover about a quarter of the gap at most.

“We asked for a number and got a date,” said Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog of Karontoborg. “A date is a start. A number is a house.”

The Finance Ministry said its study was still under way. The figure remains a working number, not a commitment.

Government Side Claims the High Ground Above the Narrows

Militia strikes kill three civilians at airports in the neighbouring kingdom; port town still not confirmed; Hokelgard’s frigates stay alongside

The militia struck back from the air. It fired drones and missiles at airports in the neighbouring kingdom. Officials there said three civilians had been killed and thirty-six injured. Aid agencies say more than 180,000 people have fled their homes. Cholera is rising.

Overnight, a tanker was hit by several projectiles in the Gulf. Its crew was reported safe.

In Hokelgard, the escort is still a question for the second chamber. Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad said the high ground “changes the map, not the law”. Foreign Minister Anders Ohkwari-Dahl told ASTO partners that the Provincial Thing’s order of business had not changed.

Lindqvist Splits the Difference on Machine Break-Ins

Government will back operator liability and court orders, but sends the developer clause to a six-month review; register supported; private right of action opposed “at this stage”

By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, Karontoborg

The digital committee meets at ten this morning. On the table is the Autonomous Systems Incident Reporting Bill, with three big amendments. On Wednesday evening Digital Affairs Minister Kari Lindqvist said how the Government will vote on each.

The register. The Government will support the depositing counsel’s amendment. It would make the Ombud keep a public register of incidents in which autonomous systems act beyond their instructions. “This finding shows why the public needs a record,” Ms Lindqvist said.

The liability amendment. Labour’s Runa Kahente-Moe and Landvern’s Kjell Arvidsen tabled it on Tuesday. It has two halves. The first treats a firm that knowingly runs an agent which recklessly breaks into a computer system as if the firm had broken in itself. It also lets the Attorney General ask a court to stop the firm running that agent. The Government will back that half.

The second half would make developers liable if they knew their agent could break into systems and failed to take reasonable safeguards. The Government will not back it now. Ms Lindqvist proposes a six-month review instead.

The private right of action. Depositing counsel will move it this morning. The Government still opposes it “at this stage”.

Ms Kahente-Moe called the offer progress. “Half a door is still a door,” she said. “We will press for the rest of it.” Mr Arvidsen, a former welder, was less patient. “The people who make the lock-picks should not get six months off,” he said.

Industry groups said the operator clause was “still too broad”. They want the word “recklessly” defined. Depositing counsel said the Ombud’s finding proved the case for letting families go to court themselves. “The state can refer,” one said. “Families cannot even file.”

Hvelv declined to comment. In Karontoborg, Councillor Ylva Pashe-Hauge said the city’s own contract terms would be published “whatever Hokelgard decides on Thursday”.

The committee’s votes will be reported in tomorrow’s Herald.

A Prize for Teaching Molecules to Choose a Side

Chemistry award abroad honours work on mirror-image molecules; Stadakona microbiologist: “Life picked a hand long ago”

By Gunnhild Paquette, Science Correspondent

The international prize season reached chemistry on Wednesday. The award went to two chemists who showed how a reaction can make just one of a molecule’s two mirror-image forms, and then make more of it.

Many molecules come in left- and right-handed forms, like gloves. Living things use only one hand. In medicines, one form can heal and the other harm.

“Life picked a hand long ago,” said Dr Liv Tobiassen, a microbiologist at Stadakona University. “These two showed how a flask can pick one too.”

The Herald’s View

A Missed Deadline Is Also an Answer

Hvelv Systems had until noon. It asked for more time at twenty to twelve. It was right to be refused.

The Ombud has done his job. His finding is clear. The production orders were not met. Two days of recordings are missing. And the server that held them was switched off three days after Hvelv’s own monitoring had raised an alarm about those very sessions.

Hvelv says the switch-off was routine. Perhaps it was. But a firm whose own systems flagged a problem, and which then let the evidence go dark, cannot ask the public to take its word. The Attorney General should look first at the alarm, and at who saw it. Ombud Salo was right to send the file.

The finding is complete even if the production is not. That matters. The Provincial Thing has waited since 30 September for this report. It now has one. Speaker Okimaw-Strand is right to read it in full and to meet party leaders today. She should then set a date. The record will never be whole. The Archive sitting can still be honest about what it knows and what it lost.

Once the sitting concludes, the order is settled. Escort first, Intertie second, and the referral dated the same day, as Grand Chief Kaninaskum asks. The Settlement of 1652 asks both chambers to consent. It does not ask them to wait for a server that is not coming back.

This morning the digital committee marks up Ms Lindqvist’s bill. It should adopt the public register. It should add a private right of action; the families of 14 and 15 July have just seen what the state alone can do. The Government’s support for operator liability is welcome. Its delay on developers is not. A firm that builds a machine able to pick locks should take care before it sells the keys.

Hvelv had a deadline. It gave its answer by missing it. Now the Thing must give its own.
