## Treasury Publishes Its Half-Weight Method

### Paper says federal sites count at half because homes on them start about twice as late; no cap; Oyelaran-Skog: "A method about the past"; working group meets on Thursday

*By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor*

The Treasury has published its method. The paper appeared on Monday afternoon, three days before the deadline it set itself.

It explains why federal land counts at half weight toward the cities' housing targets. The answer is time. Homes on federal sites, the paper says, have started about twice as late as homes on city land, chiefly because the sites lack water, sewers and roads.

"Half weight is a judgment about time, not about value," the paper says. It does not propose a cap.

Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog of Karontoborg was not persuaded. "Now we have a method," she said. "It is a method about the past."

# PAGE THREE — KARONTOBORG & THE ECONOMY

## The Method, Line by Line

### Treasury looked at ten years of federal land used for housing; first homes came about twice as late as on city land; paper rejects a cap; cities will argue on Thursday that serviced sites should count in full

*By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor*

The Treasury's paper on "half weight" is short. It runs to eleven pages. It was published on Monday afternoon, ahead of the promise to publish "before 12 November".

It answers one question. Why should a hectare of federal land count as only half a hectare toward a city's first-year housing target?

### Time, not value

The Treasury looked at federal land sold or leased for housing over the past ten years. On average, it says, the first homes on those sites started about twice as late as on land the cities found for themselves.

The paper gives three main reasons. Most federal sites had no water or sewer connection. Many had no road fit for building traffic. Some needed old buildings cleared or the ground cleaned.

"Half weight is a judgment about time, not about value," the paper says. "A hectare that will carry homes in four years is worth half as much, to a one-year target, as a hectare that will carry them in two."

### No cap

The paper does not propose a cap. Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog has asked for federal sites to count in full, with a limit. No city, in her plan, could meet more than a third of its first-year target from federal land.

The Treasury says a cap is not needed. Half weight already limits how much a city can lean on federal land, it argues. A second limit would "count the same problem twice".

### The mayor's answer

Ms Oyelaran-Skog read the paper on Monday evening. She was polite about the fact of it and sharp about the content.

"Now we have a method," she said. "It is a method about the past. It looks at sites that waited years for pipes. Some of the sites on this list have pipes today."

She said Karontoborg would argue on Thursday that a federal site with water, sewers and a road already in place should count in full. "If the problem is time," she said, "then where there is no delay there should be no discount."

Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie of Laukvik gave no view on Monday.

### What happens next

The housing working group meets on Thursday 12 November. Fifty federal sites, covering 613 hectares, are on the Treasury's list. Thirteen go to the claims table on Friday 13 November. The other thirty-seven are unclaimed.

# PAGE SIX — SCIENCE & TECHNOLOGY

## A Dinosaur Comes to Hokelgard

### Commonwealth Museum of Natural History buys a meat-eating dinosaur skeleton, the only known specimen of its kind, for VKR 26 million; it was withdrawn from auction after the deal

*By Gunnhild Paquette, Science Correspondent*

The Commonwealth Museum of Natural History in Hokelgard has bought a dinosaur.

The skeleton is that of a meat-eating dinosaur more than 150 million years old. It is the only known specimen of its species. The museum paid VKR 26 million, it said on Monday.

The skeleton was due to be sold at auction in Karontoborg. It was withdrawn from the sale after the museum agreed a price with the seller.

"A thing like this should not go into a private room," the museum's director said. "There is only one. Scientists need to be able to measure it. Children need to be able to stand under it."

### Why it matters

Palaeontologists have known about the skeleton for some years. It sat in a private collection. Only a few scientists had seen it.

A specimen that is the only one of its kind is called a type specimen. Every later find of the same animal is compared with it. If it is locked away, the work stops.

"Now it can be studied properly," the museum said. "That is the point."

### When it can be seen

The skeleton will go on show next year. The museum gave no date. It said the bones would first be cleaned, checked and mounted.

# PAGE NINE — OPINION

## THE HERALD'S VIEW

### A Paper, an Escort and a Cut

Monday was a better day than most this autumn. Three things went the right way. None of them settles anything. All of them are worth noting.

### The paper

The Treasury published its method. It did so three days early. We asked for it for weeks. We said so plainly, and the Treasury has answered plainly. That deserves credit.

The paper is short and clear. Its case is that federal land is slow. Sites have lacked pipes and roads, so homes on them have started late. Half weight, it says, is a judgment about time.

That is a real argument. It is also open to a real reply, and Mayor Oyelaran-Skog has made it. If the problem is time, a site with pipes and a road already in place has no problem. It should count in full. The Treasury's figure looks at the past. The cities need a rule that looks at each site.

On Thursday the working group can now argue about reasons, not just a number. That is progress. We hope the Treasury comes ready to move.

### The escort

The second escort is under way. VNS *Gudrid* is leading five merchant ships north. The first escort was, in the Navy's word, dull. We hope for a second dull week.

The Navy kept its promise. It said it would announce the escort when it was under way, and it did. That is not secrecy. It is good sense.

### The cut

The war-risk houses cut their rates for the first time. Shipowners will feel it. But Ms Holme is right. A lower price for a risk is not the end of the risk. The count on the long route rose to thirty on Monday. The strait will be judged by ships that come out the far side. So far there are none.

A foreign government says new strikes are "unlikely". We hope it means it. "Unlikely" is a word, not a guarantee. The mediators still have not published the text.

### The minister

This morning the Defence Minister answers the defence committee. We said yesterday that she should explain how the north will be watched the next time a cutter is in port. We say it again. The north needs an account of that day, and a plan for the next one. *Markland* returns on 19 November. A plan should not depend on one ship.

### The contract

Ten days remain to Friday 20 November. Labour asks who holds the pen. So do we. The Treasury has shown on Monday that it can publish when it chooses. It should choose again.
