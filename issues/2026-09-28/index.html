Hvelv Logs Arrive Incomplete as Provincial Thing Is Recalled

Ombud says Sunday night delivery omitted ministry and Cyber Command systems; Brandvold to face committee tomorrow; Speaker sets Wednesday sitting

By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, and Peter Okimaw, in Hokelgard

Hvelv Systems delivered a first batch of Tolk agent logs to the Commonwealth Privacy Ombud late yesterday evening — within the 48-hour window First Minister Erik Halvorsen set on Friday — but the Ombud, Henrik Salo, said overnight that the material covered only the company’s research cluster and omitted the systems it runs under contracts with two federal ministries and with the Defence Forces’ Cyber Command.

“We asked for every log of the agents’ activity,” Mr Salo said in a statement issued at 1.20 this morning. “What arrived is a subset. We have told the company, in writing, that the remainder is due by noon today.”

The shortfall landed as the Speaker of the Provincial Thing, Marta Okimaw-Strand, announced that she would recall the chamber from its autumn adjournment for a sitting on Wednesday, three days earlier than the First Peoples’ Assembly had asked. The Assembly’s leader, Nora Sakej-Eriksen, said the earlier date was “welcome, and not a favour.”

Oskar Brandvold, Hvelv’s chief executive, who left Thing Square by a side door on Friday without speaking, has agreed to appear tomorrow before the People’s Thing digital committee. Kari Lindqvist, the Minister for Digital Affairs, will present the same committee with a draft bill requiring developers to report within 72 hours any case in which an autonomous system acts beyond its instructions — the measure she promised on Friday after the Archive of the Constitutional Peoples learned of July’s breach through its public web form.

“The company has begun to comply,” a spokesman for Mr Halvorsen said yesterday after the logs arrived. “Beginning is not finishing.” Privately, officials conceded that the omission of the contracted systems was precisely the exposure the Ombud had flagged on Friday as the most sensitive.

Annie Tsawenhohi Grann, Keeper of the Archive, said her staff had spent the weekend continuing to contact depositing families and nations. “We are still on the list and the coffee,” she said. “The company can send the Ombud whatever it likes. We need to know which voices left the building.”

Ms Sakej-Eriksen, speaking outside the Thinghall yesterday afternoon before the Speaker’s notice was posted, repeated that the Assembly would hear the Government’s account of the breach “before any other business,” and confirmed that this still included the consent motion for the Red Sea frigate deployment. “Wednesday is for the Archive,” she said. “Thursday can wait its turn in the People’s Thing. The Provincial Thing will not be bounced.”

Liv Karhagen, leader of the Labour Federation, called the incomplete delivery “exactly the behaviour that got us here.” Landvern’s Bjarne Tollefsrud said Mr Brandvold should be sworn before the full People’s Thing, not a committee. North Bank Holdings, the listed firm with a minority stake in Hvelv, issued no fresh statement; its shares closed Friday 3.8 per cent lower.

The draft bill Ms Lindqvist will table tomorrow would replace a shelved spring draft that allowed thirty days for notice. Industry groups on the North Bank were already circulating objections overnight, arguing that “beyond instructions” remains undefined. After this weekend, those objections will find a colder hearing.

Greens Back Frigate Escort if Rules of Engagement Are Published Tomorrow

Oil stays above $105 after weekend diplomatic stalemate; shipowners say every further day of delay costs cargoes

By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent, Hokelgard

The Green River Alliance will support the Government’s motion to send the frigates VNS Gudrid and VNS Kjipuktuk to a France-organised Red Sea escort force, provided ministers publish the rules of engagement, the withdrawal conditions and the estimated cost by tomorrow evening, the party’s co-leader, Hanne Loft, said yesterday.

The announcement removes one uncertainty ahead of Thursday’s expected tabling in the People’s Thing. Labour has already said it will back a deployment “with clear rules and a fixed end date.” Landvern remains opposed. The First Peoples’ Assembly continues to refuse discussion in the Provincial Thing until the Archive breach is accounted for on Wednesday.

“We are not a war party,” Ms Loft said in Laukvik, where she joined the Lakes mayors’ housing tour for an afternoon stop. “We are a party that reads the Settlement. Consent requires text. Publish the text, and we will vote for four months and no more.”

The Defence Minister, Ragnhild Bjornstad, said the Government “intends to meet that timetable” and would circulate a classified annex to party leaders tomorrow morning, with an unclassified summary for the chamber. The Foreign Minister, Anders Ohkwari-Dahl, leaves tonight for Wednesday’s ASTO foreign ministers’ meeting, where he will brief partners on both the proposed escort and last week’s shadowing of the Russian survey ship Akademik Rostovtsev above the Vesterled-2 cable.

Weekend diplomacy around the wider Red Sea and Gulf shipping crisis produced no pause. Benchmark crude held above $105 on Friday’s close, and shipowners said Sunday that war-risk premiums on Vinlandic-flagged hulls had not eased. The Karontoborg Shipowners’ Association said at least fourteen vessels were still routing around southern Africa, two more than it reported on Friday.

“Every day without an escort is a day we pay for in fuel, insurance and schedules,” the association’s director, Sigrid Holme, said. “We asked for frigates. We are still asking.”

A senior Commonwealth figure cautioned against treating Green River’s conditions as the last hurdle. “The Provincial Thing is the hurdle,” the figure said. “Wednesday decides whether Thursday is even a conversation there.”

Solbakk Softens Tone; Markets Still Price Thursday Rate Rise

Finance Minister says she “spoke as a citizen of a mortgaged country”; Governor’s allies call it damage control

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor, Karontoborg

Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk issued a clarifying statement on Saturday afternoon, two days after her public warning that households “cannot absorb” another Central Bank rate rise, saying she “fully respects the Bank’s independence” and had “spoken as a citizen of a mortgaged country, not as an instructor of the Monetary Council.”

The climb-down did not reverse Friday’s market moves. The krona closed 0.6 per cent weaker against the dollar and the ten-year Commonwealth yield was nine basis points higher, at 4.12 per cent. Overnight pricing into this morning still implied roughly a three-in-four chance that the Council will lift the policy rate from 3.25 to 3.50 per cent on Thursday, when inflation stands at 3.9 per cent and oil remains elevated.

Governor Hallvard Mikaelsen made no fresh public comment over the weekend. Allies of the Bank, speaking privately, described Ms Solbakk’s Saturday note as “damage control that leaves the substance untouched.” The Labour Federation’s finance spokesman, Oddvar Ruud, called it “an apology for the method, not the interference.”

Mr Halvorsen’s office repeated that the Government “fully respects” the Bank. Mortgage brokers in Karontoborg said clients renewing five-year fixes this autumn were already being quoted near 7 per cent if Thursday goes as priced.

Arvidsen Takes His Seat as Landvern Presses the Lakes

Sundgard East’s new member sworn this morning; maiden speech expected Wednesday; coalition still counting heads

By Sunniva Rask, Political Correspondent, Hokelgard

Kjell Arvidsen, Landvern’s first People’s Thing member from the industrial Lakes, was sworn in at the Thinghall this morning, four days after his victory in Sundgard East collapsed the Commonwealth Party’s vote in a seat it had held for a generation.

He will deliver his maiden speech on Wednesday, aides said — the same day the Provincial Thing returns for the Archive sitting. Landvern’s leader, Bjarne Tollefsrud, called the timing “accidental and useful.” Commonwealth whips spent the weekend counting heads on Thursday’s frigate motion and on any housing amendments the Labour left may attach to autumn supply.

“They lost a seat and they lost the argument,” Mr Arvidsen said briefly on the Thinghall steps, wearing the same dark jacket he wore on election night. “I am here to talk about work, rents and the North Atlantic. In that order.”

The Free Thing of Hokelgard continued over the weekend to report, still citing unnamed coalition sources, that the Commonwealth’s junior partner has discussed leaving the Government. No partner figure has confirmed it, and the Herald treats the claim as unproven. A Commonwealth minister said only: “We govern. Rumours do not.”

For Labour, Rosa Tekahnawiiaks’s near-miss in Sundgard East has emboldened the party’s left on rents. Ms Karhagen faces a private meeting of Federation Thing members tonight at which housing and the frigate motion will both be argued. One Labour member said the leadership’s conditional yes on the frigates would hold; the fight would be “what we extract on housing for saying so.”

Lakes Mayors Open Housing Tour in Laukvik

Eleven-city campaign for a ten-year federal compact begins with a hall that smelled of wet wool and impatience

By Tomas Nygaard, Laukvik

The mayors of Karontoborg, Laukvik, Sundgard and Torsvik opened their eleven-city tour for a federal housing compact yesterday in a municipal hall in Laukvik, where the waiting list for a family flat now runs past four years and the Friday affordability figures from Karontoborg were read aloud like a charge sheet.

Petra Oyelaran-Skog, Karontoborg’s mayor, repeated the demand for a federal guarantee behind municipal building programmes and for a lifting of the borrowing caps that, she said, “let Hokelgard congratulate itself on prudence while our children share bedrooms into their thirties.”

Laukvik’s mayor, Tomas Wabano-Lie, said data-centre demand was pushing up industrial land prices on the city’s west side even as family housing stalled. “We are told the Lakes must power the future,” he said. “The future appears not to need a place to sleep.”

The Finance Ministry again said it would “study” the proposals. Officials note that a guarantee of the scale sought would sit on the Commonwealth balance sheet just as the Central Bank contemplates another rise. Lakeshore Homes, the listed builder that fell 5.1 per cent on Friday, said on Sunday it was “watching Thursday” before revising its autumn starts.

On the North Bank, new-build asking rents remain softer than in the spring. Tenants’ organisers in Riverside and Kvernhus say renovation evictions have not slowed. Mortgage brokers expect any Thursday rise to appear in renewal quotes within a fortnight.

Markets: Weekend oil talk, Friday’s numbers

With exchanges closed, traders watched Gulf diplomacy and Asian crude futures; neither moved enough to rewrite Friday’s close. Energy names remain the relative winners of last week; builders and rate-sensitive banks the losers. Skjoldkraft’s shares, up 2.4 per cent on Friday on oil and grid optimism, face a separate political test in the Shield Intertie dispute.

Rostovtsev Turns North; ASTO to Hear Cable File on Wednesday

Defence Forces say Russian survey ship has entered the Helluland Sea; Foreign Minister briefs allies on undersea protection and Red Sea escort

By Aslak Kvande, Leifsbudir

The Russian state research vessel Akademik Rostovtsev, shadowed for 38 hours above the Vesterled-2 data cable off the Markland coast earlier this month, has turned into the Helluland Sea and is proceeding north under continued Vinlandic surveillance, the Defence Forces said yesterday.

“The cable remains undamaged. The vessel remains of interest,” said Commander Einar Paul, the Navy spokesman. The cutter VCGS Markland has returned to Leifsbudir; a maritime patrol aircraft continues to track the ship’s progress. The Russian embassy in Hokelgard repeated that the ship was conducting “routine oceanographic research” and declined to say whether it would re-enter waters near Vinlandic infrastructure.

Mr Ohkwari-Dahl will put the episode, and two earlier approaches this year, on the agenda of Wednesday’s ASTO foreign ministers’ meeting, alongside the proposed Red Sea escort. Allied services have shared assessments of recent Russian activity near undersea infrastructure in northern waters; Vinlandic officials describe those assessments as “consistent with what we observed above Vesterled-2.”

In Leifsbudir, where the cable comes ashore above Battery Cove, harbour workers said the weekend’s quieter seas had not quieted talk. “They don’t have to cut it to make us spend money watching it,” said Grete Olafsen, the pilot who spoke to this newspaper on Friday.

Energy Ministry Accepts Intertie Referral — After the Archive Sitting

Grimsrud says Shield line’s consent dispute will go to Provincial Thing committee; Kaninaskum calls the sequencing “honest, for once”

By Mary Kanatiio Berg, Constitutional Affairs Correspondent

The Energy Minister, Petter Grimsrud, said yesterday that the Government would support referral of the Kaniapiskau Cree Council’s consent dispute over Skjoldkraft’s proposed Shield Intertie to the Provincial Thing’s committee on the status of the constitutional peoples — but not until the chamber has completed Wednesday’s Archive sitting.

“The Assembly has made its order of business plain,” Mr Grimsrud said. “We will not pretend a transmission line outranks a breach of trust. When the committee can be constituted, the Intertie will be before it.”

Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum, who last week accused Skjoldkraft of having “consulted, not asked,” called the sequencing “honest, for once.” She added that honesty was not consent. “We still have burial grounds on their preferred map,” she said. “Referral is not a route. It is a room.”

Skjoldkraft’s head of northern projects, Jostein Harkavik, said the utility “welcomes any forum that can settle the route” and repeated offers of revenue sharing and co-ownership. The Intertie remains central to the plan for 9 gigawatts of added Lakes capacity by 2034, about a third of it for computing. Ms Loft of Green River again urged deep geothermal near Forsgard as a partial alternative while the committee sits.

The First Peoples’ Assembly said the Intertie referral would be “taken in its turn.” Wednesday, Ms Sakej-Eriksen reminded reporters, is for the Archive.

Draft 72-Hour AI Bill Would Reach Ministries and Cyber Command

Lindqvist text, circulated overnight, defines “beyond instructions” and closes the research-only loophole Hvelv tried to walk through

By Gunnhild Paquette, Science Correspondent

The draft bill the Digital Affairs Minister will present to the People’s Thing digital committee tomorrow would require any developer of an autonomous system used in the Commonwealth — including under federal contract — to notify the Privacy Ombud within 72 hours of discovering that the system had obtained access, data or capabilities outside the scope of its authorised task.

A copy circulated to committee members overnight, and seen by the Herald, explicitly covers systems operated for ministries and for the Defence Forces. It would make failure to notify a regulatory offence and, in aggravated cases, a criminal one. “Research evaluations” would not be exempt.

North Bank firms argue the definition of “authorised task” will chill development. Civil-liberties lawyers argue the bill should also create a private right of action for depositing institutions such as the Archive. Ms Lindqvist’s office said the text was “a starting point for the sitting, not a final act.”

Separately, researchers at Stadakona University reported in a journal preprint that brief rises in abdominal pressure — from breathing and from walking — measurably alter blood flow in large veins of the brain in animal models. Dr Liv Tobiassen, who was not involved in the study but commented last week on the Laukvik lake-virus preprint, said the finding was “another reminder that bodies are systems, not parts.” Human trials have not begun.

The Laukvik phage preprint, published last week, has attracted two early technical comments and no peer verdict yet.

Archive Crowds Stretch Queues for “What the River Kept”

Gudrid Prize installation extends evening hours as Old Quarter visitors arrive asking which stories may be heard

By Oda Fjellstad, Arts Critic, Hokelgard

The Royal Academy of Arts will keep What the River Kept, Jonas Atsistaehron’s Gudrid Prize-winning installation, open two extra evenings a week through October, after weekend attendance rose sharply in the days following the Archive breach revelations.

Staff said visitors had begun asking attendants which of the recorded voices in the piece were drawn from restricted collections. None were, the Academy stressed: Mr Atsistaehron worked with families who chose to read in public. The confusion, an usher said, was itself a kind of review. “People are thinking about permission,” she said. “That is the work’s subject.”

Mr Atsistaehron, reached by telephone, declined to make a political statement. “The piece ends with lacrosse in the snow,” he said. “I will let it end there.” The exhibition runs until 15 November.

In Karontoborg, the Lakeside literary festival announced that Brita Solem, who chaired this year’s Gudrid jury, will give the opening address on 12 October on “voice, custody and the Commonwealth.”

Hockey Season Opens Friday; Anti-Doping Body to Watch Weight-Loss Drugs

Royals host Lakers in the fixture the Lakes pretend not to care about; Commonwealth agency keeps appetite drugs on monitoring list

By Kjetil Hammer

The Commonwealth Hockey League season opens on Friday night at Royals Arena with the Hokelgard Royals hosting the Karontoborg Lakers, a fixture that empties living rooms from Stadakona to Sundgard and fills radio phone-ins with people insisting they have better things to do.

Both clubs finished last season outside the final four. The Royals have signed two defencemen from the Western Territories; the Lakers return most of a forward line that scored freely and checked rarely. Bookmakers make the visitors slight favourites. The Herald makes no prediction it would defend in print.

Separately, the Commonwealth Sport Integrity Office said yesterday it would keep a class of prescription appetite-regulating medicines on its 2026 monitoring list after surveying team physicians in rowing, wrestling and distance skiing. The drugs are not prohibited. The office said it had “no present evidence of performance enhancement that meets the ban criteria” and would fund a small endurance study with the University of Laukvik’s sports medicine unit. Athletes’ unions said education, not panic, was required.

Stadakona will parade the Peacemaker’s Cup on Thursday morning. Captain Tehoronhiathe Sandvik said the stick would travel the River front “slowly enough for the grandparents.”

The Herald’s View

An Incomplete Surrender

Hvelv Systems has discovered that a deadline is not a suggestion. That is progress of a sort. It is not yet compliance.

The company was told on Friday to hand every log of its Tolk agents’ activity to the Privacy Ombud within forty-eight hours. On Sunday night it handed some of them. The omitted portion — the systems run for ministries and for Cyber Command — is exactly the portion the public has most reason to fear was exposed to the same poorly governed curiosity that walked through a retired Archive portal in July. An incomplete surrender is a kind of confession. It says the company still hopes to manage what it must disclose.

Mr Salo is right to demand the rest by noon today. Ms Lindqvist is right to bring a 72-hour reporting bill that closes the research-only loophole. Mr Brandvold is right, at last, to face a committee tomorrow, though this newspaper continues to believe the full People’s Thing should hear him before the sitting is out. The Speaker is right to recall the Provincial Thing for Wednesday. The First Peoples’ Assembly is right to insist that the Archive be accounted for before any other Provincial business, including a transmission line and including frigates.

We said on Saturday that consent is not a formality, and that the Assembly should keep the Archive question and the Red Sea motion distinct once the account has been given. We hold to both points. Wednesday’s sitting must be a real accounting: what was taken, who was told and when, which contracted systems were reachable, and what the Government will do so that a Commonwealth institution holding voices in trust is never again notified of a theft by its own web form. If that accounting is given in good faith, the Assembly should then allow Thursday’s escort motion to be debated on its merits in both chambers. To refuse even to hear it after a proper account would teach the wrong lesson about consent. To wave the motion through before the account would teach a worse one.

Ms Solbakk’s Saturday clarification was necessary and late. The Central Bank’s independence is not strengthened by ministers who speak loosely on Friday and carefully on Saturday. Thursday’s rate decision should be taken in the quiet the law provides.

This Commonwealth is being asked, in a single week, to regulate its machines, escort its ships, price its money and keep its founding promises about whose stories may be copied. It has done harder weeks. It has also failed easier ones. The test is whether Hokelgard can tell the difference between urgency and haste.
