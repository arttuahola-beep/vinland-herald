## Twenty-One Kilometres and 1.1 Billion: The Price of the Second Line

### Skjoldkraft puts figures on its alternative Intertie route on the eve of the status committee; Cree Council: "Better, and not enough"; Kaninaskum will ask for a consent condition on trapping grounds; committee sits this morning

*By Mary Kanatiio Berg and Sunniva Rask, Hokelgard*

The second line has a price.

On Wednesday evening Skjoldkraft filed its written evidence with the clerk of the Provincial Thing's status committee. The papers say the alternative route for the Shield Intertie is twenty-one kilometres longer than the first. It would cost about VKR 1.1 billion more to build.

The committee sits this morning in Hokelgard. It will hear the Kaniapiskau Cree Council first, then Skjoldkraft, then officials from the Energy Ministry.

The Cree Council gave its answer to the second route on Wednesday afternoon. "It is better," said Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum. "And it is not enough."

The alternative keeps wider of both burial sites. But it still crosses two of the trapping grounds the Council has named. Ms Kaninaskum said she would ask the committee to add a condition: no line through a named trapping ground without the consent of the families who use it.

"We are not asking the committee to pick a line," she said. "We are asking it to say who must agree."

First Minister Erik Halvorsen spoke briefly about the Intertie on Wednesday. He said the Government would "take the committee's finding as it comes".

# PAGE TWO — THE GREAT THING

## Third Reading Today, With a Third Meeting Behind It

### Landvern confirms it met the junior coalition partner on Wednesday; the partner says through the coalition whip that it will vote for the bill; Landvern still does not explain Tuesday's abstention; Labour to move to recommit

*By Sunniva Rask and Peter Okimaw, Hokelgard*

The meeting that Landvern could not date on Tuesday took place on Wednesday.

A Landvern spokesman confirmed on Wednesday evening that the party's leadership had met the junior coalition partner for a third time. The meeting was held in Hokelgard in the afternoon. It lasted about an hour. The spokesman would not say who attended or what was discussed. "It was a meeting," he said. "There will be others."

Within two hours the senior Commonwealth whip issued a short statement on the junior partner's behalf. It said all coalition members would vote for the Incident Reporting Bill at third reading today. "The coalition's position has not changed," it said.

The junior partner did not speak for itself. It did not deny the meeting. The Herald has still not been able to confirm the party's identity and does not print rumour as fact.

Bjarne Tollefsrud again did not explain why his party abstained on Tuesday's amendment. Asked whether Landvern would abstain again today, a spokesman said the party "will vote as it sees fit, when it sees fit".

Liv Karhagen, Leader of the Opposition, said the statement answered the wrong question. "We know how they will vote today," she said. "We still do not know what they are being offered for tomorrow."

Labour will move today to send the bill back to committee on one point: the date the public register opens. The motion is not expected to pass. "It will put every member on the record," said Runa Kahente-Moe, Labour's digital affairs spokeswoman.

Hanne Loft of the Green River Alliance said three meetings were "no longer a coincidence". "It is a habit," she said. "Habits become arrangements."

The coalition's majority on Tuesday was nine. The third reading is expected in the early afternoon.

### Minutes delivered

The Attorney General's office confirmed on Wednesday afternoon that it had received the minutes of Hvelv Systems' board review of alert escalation.

The minutes were the last document the office had asked for under the preservation notice served on Friday 9 October. The board had thirty days, to Monday 9 November. It delivered with eighteen days to spare.

The office said its lawyers would now review the minutes "with the logs and recordings already held". It did not say how long that would take. It will not publish the minutes and would not describe them.

A Hvelv spokeswoman said the board had "met its obligation in full and early". Oskar Brandvold, the chief executive, was not seen in public.

Nora Sakej-Eriksen of the First Peoples' Assembly said delivery was "a step, not an ending". "A minute tells you what a board said," she said. "It does not tell you what a machine did."

The preservation notice stays in force.

# PAGE THREE — KARONTOBORG & THE ECONOMY

## Assembly Asks for Thirteen Sites to Be Set Aside

### First Peoples' Assembly writes to the housing working group: claimed land should go to a separate table with the nations concerned; Treasury still silent on the cities' land test; mayors keep drawing

*By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor*

The First Peoples' Assembly has asked for the thirteen claimed sites on Treasury's land list to be taken out of the housing talks.

In a letter sent on Wednesday to the chairs of the housing working group, the Assembly's leader, Nora Sakej-Eriksen, asked that the sites be "set aside, in writing, before the first word about terms". She asked for a separate table, with the nations concerned and federal officials, to settle each claim.

"Those sites are not surplus," she said again on Wednesday. "They are unfinished business under agreements older than the departments that hold them. You do not bargain over unfinished business. You finish it."

The thirteen sites are among fifty published by Treasury on Tuesday. Together the fifty cover 613 hectares in the Lakes cities. The claimed sites include land beside one lake terminal and part of a former depot. Treasury has not given their combined area.

The Finance Ministry acknowledged the letter. It said Minister Ingrid Solbakk would reply "before the group meets". The working group sits on Thursday 29 October. Treasury has said terms will be its first business.

Treasury gave no answer on Wednesday to the question Labour asked on Tuesday: whether federal land will count toward the cities' land test. Liv Karhagen repeated it. "A list is a start," she said. "The rule for counting it is the finish."

### The mayors keep drawing

Karontoborg officials spent Wednesday matching Treasury's sites with the city's own plans. Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog said the rail yards east of the river "could hold a neighbourhood, if the terms let it".

She said she had no objection to the Assembly's request. "I would rather build on land that nobody disputes," she said. "That is faster in the end."

Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie of Laukvik said his city had "no claimed site to argue about and no large site to argue for". "We will take what is clean and small and start on Monday," he said.

### Help for the works

The Commonwealth and the Lakes provincial governments are preparing a support package for steel, vehicle and critical-mineral firms hit by higher fuel and shipping costs since the Gulf crisis began. Two officials said it would be announced next week. Neither would give a figure. The Finance Ministry said only that "work is in hand".

## Militia Strikes an Airport in the Gulf Kingdom

### One resident killed and five injured in an evening attack on a city in the kingdom's eastern province; debris falls near an oil field; Foreign Ministry advice unchanged; war-risk rates hold; Cape count stays at twenty-three

*By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent*

The militia that holds the southern Red Sea narrows struck an international airport in the large Gulf kingdom on Wednesday evening.

The kingdom's civil aviation authority said one resident had been killed and five people injured. It said the injuries ranged from minor to serious. All the injured were taken to hospital.

The airport serves a large city in the kingdom's eastern province, the heart of its oil industry. Debris from a drone also fell near an oil field in the same province. No one was reported hurt there.

The militia has claimed attacks on the kingdom's airports and energy sites before. Most of those claims could not be checked. This one was confirmed by the kingdom itself.

A large North African state said on Wednesday that it would help defend the kingdom's territory if asked. Its leader said his forces would not attack the militia on its own ground.

In Hokelgard, the Foreign Ministry said its travel advice for the kingdom was unchanged. Vinlanders in the eastern province were asked to follow local instructions and to keep their details up to date with the embassy.

Karontoborg's war-risk underwriters left their published Gulf rates unchanged. No further Vinlandic-flagged ship switched to the Cape route. The count stays at twenty-three.

"An airport is not a ship," said Sigrid Holme of the Karontoborg Shipowners' Association. "But our members know that a militia that can reach an airport can reach a terminal. That is what they are pricing."

### Frigates on passage

The Navy again had nothing to add on VNS *Gudrid* and VNS *Kjipuktuk*. "On passage," said Commander Einar Paul. Their arrival will be announced when they arrive. The initial escort period remains four months.

Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad was not asked about the airport strike in public. Her office said the frigates' orders "cover the sea, not the shore".

### Helluland quiet

The Coast Guard reported no transit of concern in the Helluland Sea on Wednesday. VCGS *Markland* remains alongside at Leifsbudir. The draft agreement on undersea infrastructure is still unsigned.

# PAGE FIVE — NATIONS & THE SHIELD

## Two Routes, One Question

### Status committee opens this morning with the Cree Council, then Skjoldkraft, then officials; alternative route 21 km longer and VKR 1.1 billion dearer; Kaninaskum wants consent written in for trapping grounds; Halvorsen: "We will take the finding as it comes"

*By Mary Kanatiio Berg, Constitutional Affairs Correspondent*

At ten o'clock this morning, for the first time, the Provincial Thing's status committee will sit on the Shield Intertie.

It has two routes in front of it now. On Wednesday evening it also had their prices.

Skjoldkraft's written evidence, published by the committee clerk shortly after seven, sets out both. The first route is the one the company has always proposed, with 310 kilometres across Kaniapiskau Cree lands. The second, lodged on Tuesday, runs farther west for most of its length. It is twenty-one kilometres longer and would cost about VKR 1.1 billion more.

The papers also show where each line passes. The second route keeps a wider distance from both burial sites. It avoids one trapping ground the Cree Council has named. It still crosses two others.

Jostein Harkavik, Skjoldkraft's head of northern projects, said the company had "put the bill on the table so that nobody has to guess it". "One billion is real money," he said. "So are the things it buys. The committee can see both."

### "Who must agree"

Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum studied the second route overnight on Tuesday, as she had promised. She gave her answer on Wednesday afternoon, at the Cree Council's rooms near the Provincial Thing.

"It is better," she said. "We thank them for drawing it. And it is not enough."

She will ask the committee to attach one condition to any finding. No line may cross a named trapping ground without the consent of the families who hold and use it.

"We are not asking the committee to choose a line. That is not its job," she said. "We are asking it to say who must agree before a line is chosen. That is its job."

Asked about the extra cost, Ms Kaninaskum was brief. "It was not our idea to put a line there," she said. "We should not be told what it costs to move it."

The delegation from the Cree lands will speak first this morning. It brings the burial-site maps Ms Kaninaskum promised last week, with elders who can speak to them. The maps will be heard in private session. Burial sites, the Council has said many times, are not for trade.

### What the committee can do

A status committee does not pick a route. It decides whether a project touches the constitutional status of a people, and if so on what terms. Under the Restoration of 1652, that question belongs to the Provincial Thing alone.

The committee can find that status is not touched. It can find that it is touched and set conditions. Or it can find that it is touched and that no conditions would cure it. Its finding then goes to the full chamber.

Nora Sakej-Eriksen of the First Peoples' Assembly said the condition the Cree Council wants is "the oldest kind of condition there is". "It is consent," she said. "The Settlement was built on it."

### The First Minister

Erik Halvorsen spoke for less than a minute when asked about the Intertie at a Commonwealth Party meeting in Hokelgard on Wednesday.

"The committee will hear the evidence. The Government will take the finding as it comes," he said. "A pilot does not argue with the chart because he wishes the rocks were somewhere else. That is the question before us."

Energy Minister Petter Grimsrud said again that he "welcomed any route that reduces harm". He would not say whether the Government would pay the extra VKR 1.1 billion. His officials give evidence after Skjoldkraft, probably in the afternoon.

The committee's finding is not expected today. The Herald will report the first day of evidence in Friday's paper.

## Third Reading, and a Season for the Register

### Lindqvist says the public register should open in the spring; Labour to move to send the bill back on its timing; developer clause still in review; checker opens in ten days

*By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, Karontoborg*

Minister Kari Lindqvist has given the public a season.

The incident register will open ninety days after the law takes effect. Until Wednesday, nobody would say when that might be. Asked by the Herald, Ms Lindqvist said she expected the register to open "in the spring".

"I will not give a day I cannot keep," she said. "But if the Thing passes this bill tomorrow and the Provincial Thing does its part, the public will be reading the register in the spring. Operators will have been reporting since the winter."

She did not say which month. Her office said the commencement date would be set by order once the bill has passed both chambers.

The third reading is today. Labour will move to send the bill back to committee to bring the opening date forward. Runa Kahente-Moe said the Minister's answer had helped her case. "Spring is a long word," she said. "It begins in March and ends in June. Members should vote for a date, not a season."

The Lakes Digital Industry Council said it supported the bill "as amended" and would oppose any motion to recommit.

The rest of the bill stands as it left committee: a public register, operator liability, and a developer clause held in a six-month review. Depositing counsel have still not tabled their motion on a private right of action.

Karontoborg's draft contract terms for autonomous systems remain open for comment until the end of the month. Councillor Ylva Pashe-Hauge said the city would read the final bill "line by line" before closing its own consultation.

The ministry's public checker for machine-made images and audio opens on 1 November, in ten days.

# PAGE NINE — OPINION

## THE HERALD'S VIEW

### What the Committee Is For

This morning the Provincial Thing's status committee begins its work on the Shield Intertie. It now has two routes, two prices and one condition to consider. It should be clear about which of these is its business.

The prices are not. Skjoldkraft deserves credit for putting them on paper. A second route that is twenty-one kilometres longer and VKR 1.1 billion dearer is a serious offer. It keeps farther from the burial sites. It spares one trapping ground. Nobody draws that line for public relations.

But the cost of a route is a question for the company, for the Energy Minister and, in the end, for the People's Thing, which votes the money. The status committee has a narrower and older task. Under the Restoration of 1652, it must decide whether a project touches the status of a constitutional people, and on what terms. It is not a board of accountants. If it starts weighing graves against kilometres, it will have stopped doing its job.

That is why the Cree Council's request matters most. Ruth Kaninaskum is not asking the committee to choose a line. She is asking it to say who must agree before any line crosses a named trapping ground. That is exactly the kind of question the committee exists to answer. The committee should hear her case in full and test it with care. It may decide that "the families who hold and use it" needs a sharper definition. It should not decide that consent is too expensive.

Mr Halvorsen said on Wednesday that the Government will take the finding as it comes. Good. Mr Grimsrud should now say what the Government would do if the finding favours the dearer line. A minister who welcomes "any route that reduces harm" should be ready to say who pays for it.

In the People's Thing, the Lindqvist bill reaches third reading today. Ms Lindqvist has now said the register should open in the spring. That is better than nothing and worse than a date. Members who vote for the bill should ask her to name the month.

Landvern has met the junior coalition partner three times. The partner says, through someone else's mouth, that it will vote with the coalition today. That is welcome. It is not an explanation. A party in government that holds private meetings with the opposition owes the public at least one sentence in its own name.

Finally, the Hvelv minutes are with the Attorney General, eighteen days early. We said these pages would note it when it happened. It has happened. Now the review must be thorough, and it must not be slow.
