Government Adds the Lock, and a Date

Revised amendment says the standard contract will be laid before applications open and that no money is paid until it has been; Solbakk: “by Friday 20 November”; Labour will back it; Landvern still abstains; the vote is this evening

By Sunniva Rask, Hokelgard

Ministers have given Labour what it asked for. They have added a date as well.

On Monday afternoon Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk’s office tabled a new amendment to Labour’s motion. It keeps Sunday’s promise that the standard contract for the industry package will be laid before the People’s Thing before applications open. It now also carries Labour’s rule: no money is paid to any firm until the contract has been laid.

Ms Solbakk said the contract would be laid “by Friday 20 November”. No minister has given a date before.

“On Sunday I said a condition had become a compliment,” said Oddvar Ruud, Labour’s finance spokesman. “Today the compliment has become a condition.” Labour will vote for the motion as amended.

Landvern will still abstain. The Green River Alliance will vote for the amended text. The debate begins after question time today. The vote is expected this evening.

The Lock Goes In

Government’s new amendment sets the contract before applications and the money after it; Ruud: “The compliment has become a condition”; Landvern to abstain on both texts; Greens will vote for the amended motion; division expected this evening

By Sunniva Rask, Hokelgard

On Sunday ministers were going to rewrite Labour’s motion. On Monday they decided to sign it.

Labour’s motion asks the People’s Thing to resolve that no money from the VKR 14 billion industry package be paid to any firm until the standard contract has been laid before the Thing. On Sunday the Government offered a different sentence. It welcomed a commitment to lay the contract before applications open, and said nothing about payment.

The new amendment, tabled on Monday afternoon, joins the two ideas. The House would welcome the commitment to lay the contract before applications open. It would also resolve that no money be paid to any firm until the contract has been laid.

A date, outside the text

Ms Solbakk gave the date in a statement, not in the amendment. The contract would be laid “by Friday 20 November”.

That is the day after the Central Bank decides on interest rates, and three days after her autumn fiscal statement on Tuesday 17 November. Applications are due to open “before the new year”.

“Members asked for the order of things to be clear,” Ms Solbakk said. “It is now.”

First Minister Erik Halvorsen said the same thing more briefly. “The Thing will see the contract before any firm does,” he told reporters in Thing Square.

Labour

Mr Ruud welcomed the change, with care.

“On Sunday I said a condition had become a compliment. Today the compliment has become a condition,” he said. “I would have liked the date in the text. But the lock is in the text, and that was the point. We will vote for it.”

Liv Karhagen, the Labour leader, was less generous. “A government that calls a rule unnecessary on Sunday and adds it on Monday has not changed its mind,” she said. “It has counted.”

Labour will not oppose the amendment. It will vote for the motion as amended.

Landvern still abstains

Landvern will abstain on both texts, a party spokesman said on Monday.

“We have said the package is late, and that Karontoborg gets the first of it,” Bjarne Tollefsrud, the leader, said. “A lock on a late package is still a late package. Those who like it can vote for it.”

Greens

The Green River Alliance will vote for the amended text. Hanne Loft, the co-leader, said the Government had “written our sentence into its own motion”.

“We will vote for it, and then we will watch the calendar,” she said.

An opposition-day motion does not bind the Government. But a text that Government, Labour and Greens all support is hard to ignore. A Commonwealth whip said all coalition members would be present. “We did not need to count,” the whip said. “We counted anyway.”

Bishops and Minister Meet

Wapistan and the bishops talk for an hour on advance requests for an assisted death; “We disagree about one clause”; the letter stands

By Sunniva Rask

Health Minister Gunnar Wapistan met the bishops of the Vinlandic Church in Hokelgard on Monday. The meeting lasted about an hour.

The bishops said on Sunday that they “cannot support” advance requests for an assisted death. Their letter has not changed. Their office called the meeting “frank and courteous”.

“We disagree about one clause,” Mr Wapistan said afterwards. “We do not disagree about the people it is meant for.”

He repeated that no doctor, nurse or chaplain would be asked to act against conscience. He did not offer to change the clause. The Lakes and the River provinces have said they would offer advance requests if the bill passes. No other province has spoken.

A Cap of One Third

Oyelaran-Skog names her figure for federal land: full count, but no more than a third of a city’s first-year target; Wabano-Lie: “Now I have a number”; Treasury has nothing new on its method

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor

Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog of Karontoborg has given the number she would not give on Saturday.

Federal land should count in full toward a city’s land target, she said on Monday, but no city should meet more than one third of its first-year target that way. She will put the figure to the housing working group when it meets on Thursday 12 November.

“A third is generous to the city that has the rail yards and firm with the city that has the luck,” she said. “I can defend it in both places.”

Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie of Laukvik said on Saturday that he wanted to see the cap first. On Monday he saw it.

“Now I have a number,” he said. “A third is a number I can be careful about. I will ask what it does to Laukvik, and I will ask it on the twelfth, not before.”

A Treasury spokesman said the department had nothing to add. It has said it will publish “the method behind the figure”, the reasoning for half weight, before 12 November.

The thirty-seven unclaimed federal sites are still open, and the other thirteen go to the claims table on Friday 13 November. The fifty sites cover 613 hectares. The land test is one of four Ms Solbakk set for the compact on 18 October, and the VKR 3.5 to 4.5 billion housing envelope reaches its ceiling only if first-year land targets are met.

Factories Slip, and the Statement Will Hold the Line

Lakes purchasing managers’ index falls to 49.5, first reading below 50 this year; Solbakk’s office: no new spending in the autumn statement beyond the package and the housing envelope

By Elise Brattli

The Lakes Purchasing Managers’ Institute said on Monday that its factory index fell to 49.5 in October. Any reading below 50 means more firms are shrinking than growing. It is the first such reading this year. September was above 50.

Last Friday the Statistics Office said the economy did not grow in August and may have shrunk in September. Shops had a good month. Factories now look less sure.

Ms Solbakk’s office said the autumn statement on 17 November would “hold the line”. It would add no new spending beyond the industry package and the housing envelope already announced.

“That is prudent,” said Hedda Vang, chief economist at the Lakes Mortgage Institute. “It is also a decision to let the stall run. If the Central Bank raises rates two days later, the Treasury will not be helping.”

Most economists still expect a rise on Thursday 19 November from the present 3.50 per cent. Inflation is 3.9 per cent. Large Lakes lenders have held their five-year fixed rates at 7.2 per cent, except two at 7.3.

Miners and Industrials Lead a Rebound

KXC up 1.2 per cent to 20,792; chip designers lower; Glimra Compute to sell shares and a bond; crude falls 4.5 per cent; gold ends a three-day fall

By Tomas Nygaard, Karontoborg

Karontoborg shares recovered some of Friday’s loss on Monday. The KXC Composite rose 1.2 per cent to 20,792.

Mining companies and industrial firms led the gain. Gold stopped falling and rose $67 to $3,981 an ounce, its first rise in four days. Chip designers ended lower, as they did abroad.

Benchmark crude fell 4.5 per cent to $113.22 a barrel on talk of a plan to reopen the Gulf strait. Energy producers fell with it.

The ten-year Commonwealth yield fell five basis points to 3.83 per cent. The krona was a little weaker at 6.89 to the dollar and 7.96 to the euro.

Glimra goes back to the market

Glimra Compute said it would sell new shares and a new bond, each worth about VKR 1.7 billion, to pay for a third data hall near Forsgard. Its shares fell.

In October Glimra put off a VKR 2.4 billion bond for its second hall, then reopened it three days later. This time it is asking for both kinds of money at once.

“A firm that wants to build that fast takes the market as it finds it,” one trader said.

Still No Answer for the Steelworkers

By Tomas Nygaard

Sørstrand Steel gave the steelworkers’ union no written guarantee on Monday. The union asked on 29 October for a promise that no jobs will be lost while one of the Laukvik mill’s two strip lines is stopped. The pause begins on 1 December. About 380 workers will go on short time.

Quotes Return, Dearer

Both houses that stopped quoting on Friday are back on Monday; the fifth rise in about a week; Cape count now thirty; Holme: “Thirty. A number with no cheer in it”

By Tomas Nygaard

The two larger Karontoborg war-risk houses that stopped giving new Gulf quotes on Friday afternoon started again on Monday. Both had said they would.

Both raised their rates. It is the fifth rise in about a week.

“We know what the risk is now,” one underwriter said. “It is expensive.”

The pause lasted a weekend. It was the first time in the crisis that the houses had stopped quoting. Cover already written was not affected.

One more Vinlandic-flagged ship has taken the long route round the Cape, the Shipowners’ Association said. The number is now thirty.

“Thirty,” said Sigrid Holme, the Association’s director. “It is a number with no cheer in it. Each of them adds days, fuel and wages to a cargo that someone will eventually pay for.”

No Vinlandic ship was reported hit in the Gulf over the weekend or on Monday, the Association said.

Two Days, If Everyone Agrees

Officials say the strait could reopen within two days of an agreement; none announced; crude falls as traders mark down the risk; Holme: “A plan that opens in two days can close in one”

By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent

Officials in the region said on Monday that the Gulf strait could be open to shipping again within two days of an agreement among the parties. Over the weekend the talk was of a staged reopening.

No agreement has been announced. Mediators said messages were still passing between the parties. A round of talks had ended without a result.

Benchmark crude fell 4.5 per cent on Monday, to $113.22. It is still above where it stood in mid-October.

Ms Holme was not ready to celebrate.

“A plan that opens the strait in two days can close it in one,” she said. “Our members will wait and watch. A captain does not plot a route from a press briefing.”

The Committee Names a Day

Status committee will meet on Wednesday 11 November to take up the Cree Council’s written answers; Kaninaskum: “They have found a way to take one day less”; still no figure from Skjoldkraft

By Mary Kanatiio Berg, Constitutional Affairs Correspondent

The status committee has set a date. It will meet on Wednesday 11 November.

The chair’s office said on Monday that members would take up the written answers sent by the Kaniapiskau Cree Council on 29 October. It gave no date for a finding.

Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum did the arithmetic. From the day the Council replied to the day the committee will sit is thirteen days.

The answers themselves, like the record of the holders, remain private.

Skjoldkraft gave no figure on Monday for what the Shield Intertie’s second route would add to a household power bill.

Sixty Days on a Clock

Lakes government sets a comment window for nations on the road study’s terms, to 1 January; Sakej-Eriksen: “A countdown, not a consultation”

By Mary Kanatiio Berg

The Lakes government said on Monday that nations may send comments on the terms of its road study within sixty days, until 1 January.

Last week it said the nations would be “invited to comment”. Nora Sakej-Eriksen, the leader of the First Peoples’ Assembly, called that “a guest’s word”.

On Monday she called the window something else.

“It is a countdown, not a consultation,” she said. “A clock that starts before the terms are agreed is not an invitation. It is a way of saying, later, that we were asked.”

The Lakes government said the window was “generous” and could be reviewed. It did not say when.

Eighty Thousand Checks, Most With No Label

Ministry says about 80,000 checks were made on the public tool for machine-made images and audio since it opened on Sunday; “a picture with no label is not a fake picture”

By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, Karontoborg

The ministry’s public checker for machine-made images and audio has been used about 80,000 times since it opened on Sunday, the ministry said on Monday.

Most of the checks found no label at all.

“That tells people less than they might hope,” a ministry spokesman said. “A picture with no label is not a fake picture. It is a picture we cannot say anything about.”

The checker looks for hidden marks that several large developers put in their output. It cannot prove that an image is real. The ministry said the tool had run normally on Monday after Sunday’s slowdown.

The ministry will publish a fuller report after a full week. The teacher who tried the tool with her class on Sunday said the test was whether people still used it in a month. The first number is a good start. It is only a start.

A Switch Only Tumours Need

Laukvik chemists report a molecule that shuts off a growth signal in cancer cells missing a partner gene and spares healthy cells in the dish; “a good key, no door yet”

By Ingvild Sørhus

Chemists at the University of Laukvik have reported a molecule that stops a growth signal in certain tumour cells and leaves healthy cells alone.

The molecule works only in cells that lack a partner gene, which many tumours have lost. It switched off the signal in those cells in the laboratory. In ordinary cells it did little.

That is the point. A drug that hurt every cell would be a poison. One that hurts only the cells that have lost a gene might be a treatment.

“It is a good key,” said Dr Sigrun Odjig, who led the work. “We do not yet know whether it fits any door in a person. That is years away, and most keys do not fit.”

The team tested the molecule in dishes of cells, not in animals. It will take several more steps before anyone gives it to a patient.

The agency’s advice stands. Vulnerable groups should be vaccinated now.

Two Openings by the Harbour

Kjipuktuk College of Art opens Ingeborg Sylliboy’s tide-chart prints and Halvard Denny’s burnt-wood panels; both run into December

By Aslak Kvande, Kjipuktuk

The Kjipuktuk College of Art opened two shows in its harbour gallery on Monday evening. They are small, and they are worth the walk.

Ingeborg Sylliboy’s prints begin with old tide charts. She has printed the lines of the charts in dark blue on pale paper, then cut into them so that the lines break up like water in a wind. From a few steps away each print is a map. From close up it is a surface that moves.

“Every harbour on this coast has a chart that says when it is safe to come in,” Ms Sylliboy said. “I wanted to make one that says when it is not.”

Halvard Denny’s panels are made of wood, burned and then scraped back. Long pale grain shows through black. Some are as tall as a door. They are made slowly and look it.

“A fire takes a minute,” Mr Denny said. “Getting it to leave something behind takes a month.”

The prints and panels share the long room well. The blue and the black do not compete.

Both shows run until 12 December. The gallery is open to the public every afternoon except Monday.

Still running

The Royal Academy’s late openings of Jonas Atsistaehron’s What the River Kept continue on Thursday and Friday, until ten o’clock. The show closes on 15 November.

Marit Nesheim’s The Night Ferry runs at the Old Quarter Playhouse in Hokelgard until 28 November.

The Herald’s View

Now Hold Them to the Date

Ministers have done what we asked on Monday and a little more.

We said that the cleanest answer was to keep both ideas: lay the contract before firms apply, and pay nothing until it has been laid. Ms Solbakk’s office tabled exactly that on Monday afternoon. It also gave a date. That is better than the amendment of Sunday and better than Labour’s motion. Credit is due, and members should say so tonight.

But a date in a statement is not a date in a text. The amendment says only “before applications open”, and applications open “before the new year”. The day, Friday 20 November, is in Ms Solbakk’s statement. Members should ask her to repeat it from the despatch box.

Mr Ruud is right that the lock is what matters. The lock is in the text. If the date slips, the money does not move. We would not now quarrel with Labour for taking the win.

Landvern’s seat

Landvern will abstain on both texts. On Sunday Mr Tollefsrud said the two texts were two ways of signing the same cheque. Now the cheque has a lock on it. He says a lock on a late package is still a late package. That is true. It is also not a reason to stay in the lobby. A party that thinks the package is late and badly aimed has one more reason to want the terms in front of the House, not fewer.

The committee and the clock

The status committee will meet on 11 November. Thirteen days to read a letter is long, but it is a date, and the committee had none on Sunday. It should now say how it will hear the Council and when it will report.

The Lakes government’s sixty days are a different matter. A comment window that begins before the terms are settled gives the nations a clock and no agenda. Ms Sakej-Eriksen is right to call it a countdown. The Lakes government says the window could be reviewed. It should review it now, and tell the First Peoples’ Assembly when the terms will be final.

A cheaper barrel is not a safer strait

Crude fell 4.5 per cent on Monday on talk of reopening the Gulf in two days. On the same day the war-risk houses put their rates up. They were right. A plan that has not been agreed is not a passage. Shipowners should keep the Cape route in use until a tanker sails through unasked.

A statement that holds the line

The factory survey below 50, a flat August and a possible fall in September make the autumn statement harder to write. Ms Solbakk’s office says it will add no new spending. That may be prudent. But a Treasury that holds the line while the Central Bank is thinking of raising rates owes the public a plain account of what it expects from the next two quarters. The statement should give one.
