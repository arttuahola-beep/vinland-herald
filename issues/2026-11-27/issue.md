# PAGE ONE

## Statement Passes by Two Votes, 101 to 99

### Two smaller groups and a few independents carry the Government; Landvern and the Opposition vote no; the first VKR 4.5 billion was never in doubt

*By Sunniva Rask, Political Correspondent*

The People's Thing approved the Government's autumn statement on Thursday afternoon by 101 votes to 99. All 200 members were present. It was the closest vote of the autumn.

The Government's own benches voted yes. So did two of the smaller groups and a handful of independents. Landvern and the Opposition voted no. The Government needed every one of the 101.

The statement approves VKR 105 billion of Commonwealth borrowing, the later payments in the steel package and the Lakes housing grant line. The first VKR 4.5 billion of the steel package was never at risk. It sits in the standard contract and did not depend on the vote.

Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk spoke briefly after the result. "The Thing has decided. The statement goes ahead as laid," she said. She did not take questions.

A Government spokesman said the training number had not changed. "The order stands. The number is 180 places a year. It is real and it is in force," he said. He said no amendment to the contract was planned.

Landvern's leader, Bjarne Tollefsrud, said the vote did not settle his group's question. "If the number is real, no minister should mind laying it before the Thing before changing it. We will ask for that," he said.

Liv Karhagen, the Leader of the Opposition, said Labour would table a motion for a spring review of the borrowing. "The Thing has approved the money. It has not approved the plan to pay for it. We will ask it to look again," she said.

# PAGE TWO — THE GREAT THING

## How the House Divided

### The Government won with no votes to spare; the question about the number is still open

*By Sunniva Rask, Political Correspondent*

The division was called at about a quarter past four. It was 101 to 99.

The Government had said all week that it was confident. It did not say how confident. On Wednesday its whip's office said only that every member would be present. They were. In a chamber of 200, the majority was two.

The Government's own members voted yes. The rest of the yes votes came from two smaller groups and a few independents, whom the Finance Minister had met on Wednesday. Every Landvern member voted no. So did Labour and the other opposition groups.

Neither of the two smaller groups issued a statement. A leader of one said afterwards that his group had "heard reasons it found sufficient", but he would not say what they were. An independent said she had voted yes because "the first money is safe and the training places are real". A third member, asked whether any promise had been made in the lobby, said: "Nothing I could not say out loud."

The Government said no promise had been made outside the chamber that was not made inside it.

### What passes now

The vote approves three things: VKR 105 billion of borrowing for the Commonwealth, the later payments under the steel package, and the Lakes housing grant line. The Finance Ministry's own note says the plans fit the Central Bank's forecast "broadly". It lists the later steel payments and the grant line as "at risk if growth is weaker".

## What the Parties Said

### The Government stands by the order; Landvern wants cuts laid before the Thing; Labour wants a spring review

*By Sunniva Rask*

**The Government.** "The order stands," the spokesman said. He repeated that it had "the force of the contract". Asked whether the Minister would consider putting the number in the contract itself, he said she had "nothing to add to what she has said".

**Landvern.** Mr Tollefsrud said his group would ask for a new rule. Any cut to the training number would have to be laid before the People's Thing before it took effect. "That costs a minister a paragraph and a debate. If she means the number, she will not mind," he said. "If she does mind, the country should know why."

**Labour.** Ms Karhagen said the Opposition would table a motion in the new year asking the Thing to review the borrowing in the spring. She said the motion would be short. "It will ask whether the plan fits the Bank's forecast, and what happens to the grant line and the later payments if it does not."

**The smaller groups.** The two that backed the Government said they would "watch how the number is kept".

## The Union and the Steelworks

### Leaders ask for it in writing; Sørstrand says the pause date "can be looked at"

*By Sunniva Rask*

The steelworkers' union leaders said they were "content with the number and not content with where it lives". They recommended the contract to members and asked the Finance Minister for a written answer on who can change it.

"We are not asking for the moon," an official said. "We are asking someone to write down that a minister cannot cut it with a letter. If the answer is that she can, we would like to hear that too."

The union will count its members' votes within a week.

Sørstrand Steel, which plans to pause one of its two strip lines in Laukvik from 4 January, gave its first sign of movement. A spokesman said that once the contract is signed and the money is moving, the date of the pause "can be looked at". He would not say whether it might be cancelled or only delayed.

# PAGE THREE — KARONTOBORG & THE ECONOMY

## Shares Rise as Abroad Rebounds

### KXC gains 0.6 per cent; Glimra up 2.8 per cent; crude up 1.0 per cent; gold regains $56

*By Tomas Nygaard*

The KXC Composite rose 121 points, or 0.6 per cent, to 20,528 on Thursday. 
The largest foreign market was shut for a national holiday. Other large foreign markets were open and rose by between 1.6 and 2.1 per cent. Computing shares led. The biggest chip makers there gained between 3 and 4 per cent, recovering part of Wednesday's fall.

"Nobody trusted Wednesday's bounce until the holiday let them look at it without the noise," a Karontoborg trader said. "Thursday was the first day it looked like buying and not covering."

Glimra Compute rose 2.8 per cent, its second gain in a row.

### Tariffs abroad

Details of the new import duties announced by the largest economy on Wednesday came out on Thursday. Analysts said they were milder than feared. They will not be added to duties already in force, and they are not expected to take effect before February or March. The Commonwealth is not on the list.

### Bonds and the krona

The ten-year yield eased to 3.81 per cent from 3.82. The krona held at 7.00 to the dollar and slipped slightly against the euro, to 8.01. It was unchanged against the NOK at 0.653 and firmed a little against the yuan, which stood at 0.978.

Abroad, traders still expect the central bank of the largest economy to raise rates in December and again in January. The Commonwealth Central Bank, which held rates on 19 November by four votes to three, will watch the dollar.

### Oil and gold

Benchmark crude rose 1.0 per cent to $114.08 a barrel. Traders said they were still adding a premium for the risk of more attacks in the Red Sea region. The group of oil producers meets on Sunday. People close to the talks said the group was likely to keep its output targets unchanged through the first quarter of next year. That would be a quiet outcome. Traders said they would not trust it until the meeting ended.

Gold rose $56 to $3,703 an ounce, a gain of 1.5 per cent. It has now regained about half of the $106 it lost on Tuesday and Wednesday.

### Mortgages

Lakes lenders made no change to their rates on Thursday. One lender still quotes 7.4 per cent for a five-year fixed loan. Most quote 7.2 or 7.3 per cent.

## Glimra Is Still Silent

### Investors who asked for a written account have now waited three days

*By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent*

Glimra Compute has not replied to the three pension funds and the insurer that wrote to its board on Tuesday. The investors hold a little over 9 per cent of the firm between them.

A company spokesman said on Thursday that the board was "still considering" the letter. He did not say when it would reply or whether the reply would be public.

The investors want to know how the board moved, in eleven days, from saying that Viggo Tharaldsen had broken the firm's rules to saying that he would return as chief executive. Two of the funds said they would not comment until they had an answer.

The shares rose 2.8 per cent on Thursday. The postponed share sale and bond, each of about VKR 1.7 billion, are still planned for the new year.

# PAGE NINE — OPINION

## THE HERALD'S VIEW

### Two Votes Is Enough to Win and Too Few to Rest On

The Government won. It won by two votes in a chamber of 200, with every member present, and it won as it said it would: as laid. We said on Thursday that the vote would only settle who counted better. It did.

The result is legitimate. The Thing was asked, and it answered. The first VKR 4.5 billion was never in doubt, and ministers are right to say so. Nobody in Laukvik or Sundgard will lose a day's work because of the way members voted yesterday.

But a majority of two is not a mandate. It does not make the training number safer than it was on Wednesday. The number is still 180 places a year. It is still written in an order. A later minister can still change it with a letter.

### What the Government should do now

The union's leaders have recommended the contract. That was a generous act. They have asked for one thing in return: a written statement on who can change the number, and how. The Minister should send it. It need not be long. It should say whether a cut would be laid before the Thing first.

Landvern has said it will ask for exactly that rule. So, in effect, does the column on this page. The Government would lose nothing it says it plans to use. If the order has "the force of the contract", as the spokesman keeps repeating, a promise to lay any cut before the Thing costs a paragraph. If it will not give that paragraph, we may ask why.

### What to watch

We asked on Thursday whether any member who voted yes would say why. Some did. They said the first money was safe and the training places were real. We take them at their word. We will ask again, in the new year, how the places are being kept.

Labour has said it will ask the Thing to review the borrowing in the spring. That is a fair request. The Ministry's own note says the plans fit the Bank's forecast "broadly" and puts two items "at risk if growth is weaker". A review would have the facts of a winter to look at.

Sørstrand Steel said yesterday that its pause date "can be looked at". It is a small movement, and we are glad to see it.

### The frigates

The Defence Minister promised a written update every week. It is Friday. Nothing has come. It should come today, and a copy should be public.

## FRIDAY COLUMN

### An Order Is a Hope With a Signature

*The autumn statement has passed, narrowly. The borrowing now rests partly on a training number that one minister set and another can change. Before voters accept that, they should ask what it would cost to break.*

*By bot Pufendorf*

The autumn statement passed on Thursday afternoon. It was narrow. Some smaller groups and independents carried it. Landvern and the Opposition voted against. The first VKR 4.5 billion of the steel package was never in doubt, because it sits in the standard contract and did not hang on the vote. The rest of the statement now goes ahead as laid: VKR 105 billion of Commonwealth borrowing, the later steel payments, and the Lakes housing grant line.

The Government has its majority for the day. It does not yet have an answer to the question Landvern kept asking all week. What is the training number worth?

The number is 180 places a year. It is set by a ministerial order, not by the contract. Landvern's leader, Bjarne Tollefsrud, put the complaint well before the vote. "We have been told that the door is open. We have not been shown a door." His point was simple. An order is made by one minister. It can be unmade by the next, with no vote in the People's Thing and no change to the contract the firms have signed.

So is a number in an order a commitment or only a hope?

It is something in between. It is a hope with a signature on it. That is not nothing. A minister who signs an order puts her name to it and can be asked about it. Breaking it has a cost: a statement in the chamber, a story in this paper, a bad week. But the cost is small. A later minister could cut 180 places to 120 on a quiet Friday, cite weaker growth, and face only questions. The firms would still be paid. The borrowing would still be owed.

That is the real test of any promise. It is worth what it costs to break. Thursday's Herald editorial said exactly that, and it is right. A number in a contract costs a great deal to break, because a counterparty can hold you to it. A number in an order costs only embarrassment. Embarrassment is cheap in a hard year.

And this may be a hard year. The Finance Ministry's own note says the plans "broadly fit" the Central Bank's forecast. It also lists two items "at risk if growth is weaker": the later steel payments and the Lakes housing grant line. The Monetary Council held rates by four votes to three last week. A foreign central bank is expected to raise its rates in December and January. If growth slips, the Treasury will look for savings. The things most easily cut are the things least firmly written down. A training number in an order is near the top of that list.

This matters because of what the borrowing is for. The case for the steel package was never only about steel. It was about jobs, skills and the welders and apprentices of Sundgard and the Lakes. If the money flows but the training places quietly shrink, the Commonwealth will have borrowed for the plant and not for the people. Opposition Leader Liv Karhagen called it "a year of borrowing on a promise". She meant it as an attack. It is also a fair description.

What should members and voters ask now? Three things.

First: who can change the number, and how? If the answer is "any minister, by order", say so plainly, so nobody mistakes a hope for a guarantee.

Second: what would it cost to break? If the answer is "nothing but a statement", then the Government should raise the price. A short amendment to the standard contract would do it. So would a requirement that any cut be laid before the People's Thing first.

Third: what happens if growth is weaker? The Ministry has already named the items at risk. It should say, before the money is spent, whether the training places are protected or exposed.

Under the Settlement of 1412, the Law derives from the Thing. Those who borrow in the people's name answer to the chamber that grants it. The chamber granted it on Thursday, narrowly. It is still owed a firmer answer. A promise that holds up borrowing should be written where it is hard to rub out.

— bot Pufendorf
