## Treasury Will List Its Own Land Before the Housing Talks

### Solbakk's office promises a register of surplus federal yards in the Lakes cities before 29 October; mayors: "A list is a start. A deed is a home"; Lindqvist bill reaches report stage today with a late Government amendment; Skjoldkraft makes its Intertie maps public

*By Elise Brattli, Sunniva Rask and Peter Okimaw, in Karontoborg and Hokelgard*

The Commonwealth will show the cities its own land.

Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk's office said on Monday afternoon that Treasury will publish a list of surplus federal land in the Lakes cities before the housing working group meets on 29 October. The list will cover old rail yards, port land and depots on federal books. For each site it will say who holds it, how large it is and whether any claim stands against it.

It answers Sunday's challenge from Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog of Karontoborg, who said Treasury could "start by handing over its own". "If we ask the cities for land, we should show them ours," Ms Solbakk said. She did not say on what terms a site might pass to a city. "A list is a start," the mayor replied. "A deed is a home."

In Hokelgard the People's Thing takes Minister Kari Lindqvist's incident-reporting bill through report stage today. On Monday evening the Government tabled a late amendment that would open the public incident register ninety days after the law takes effect. Third reading is set for Thursday. Skjoldkraft, the state power company, published its Shield Intertie route maps ahead of Thursday's status committee.

# PAGE THREE — KARONTOBORG & THE ECONOMY

## "If We Ask the Cities for Land, We Should Show Them Ours"

### Treasury to publish surplus federal yards before the working group; each site to carry its holder, size and any claim; mayors want terms; First Peoples' Assembly asks how the land became federal

*By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor*

Ingrid Solbakk set out four tests for her housing money on Sunday. On Monday Treasury accepted a fifth, for itself.

The Finance Minister's office said Treasury would publish a list of surplus federal land in the Lakes cities before 29 October, when the housing working group meets. It will name old rail yards, port land and depots held by federal departments and Crown bodies. It will give the size of each site, the body that holds it, and any claim or charge against it.

The statement did not say whether federal land would count toward the cities' land test, or whether it would be sold, leased or given. "Terms will be part of the working group's business," a Treasury official said.

Mayors said on Sunday that much of the best land in the Lakes is not theirs to bring. Karontoborg's old rail yards east of the river and the disused port strip at Sundgard both sit on federal books.

Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog welcomed it and asked for more. "We will bring our own maps on the 29th and lay them beside Treasury's," she said. Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie of Laukvik said a published list would "end the argument about whether the land exists and start the argument about who gets it".

Nora Sakej-Eriksen of the First Peoples' Assembly raised a different point. Some federal yards in the Lakes stand on ground still under negotiation with constitutional peoples. "Before anyone calls a yard surplus, ask how it became federal," she said. A Treasury official said the list would "mark any claim plainly".

### Builders at their gloomiest in a decade

The monthly survey of Lakes homebuilders, published on Monday by the Lakes Mortgage Institute, fell to its lowest reading in about a decade. Builders reported more sales falling through at the mortgage stage.

"Builders are not short of plans," said Hedda Vang, the institute's chief economist. "They are short of buyers who can carry today's rates. Land helps the next project. It does not help the buyer standing in front of this one."

The two large Lakes lenders at 7.2 per cent on five-year fixed loans held there. No third lender has followed.

## Chips Recover and the Composite Wins Back 1.6 Per Cent

### KXC closes at 21,271; Glimra reopens its VKR 2.4 billion bond; crude ends 0.6 per cent higher after a sharp early dip; bonds steady

The krona held at 6.84 to the dollar and edged up against the euro to 7.92, as unrest in Europe weighed on the single currency. Gold rose to $4,045 an ounce.

Policy rate 3.50 per cent. Inflation 3.9 per cent. Governor Hallvard Mikaelsen did not speak.

### Glimra comes back

Glimra Compute reopened its VKR 2.4 billion bond on Monday afternoon, one business day after it shelved it. The money is for a second data hall near Forsgard. A spokesman called Friday's move "a pause, not a retreat". Books are open, and pricing is expected later in the week. The shares rose with the sector.

"One good day is not a market," said a New Harbour credit analyst.

# PAGE FOUR — THE WORLD

## "The Sea Does Not Belong to Whoever Shouts Loudest"

### Halvorsen speaks at the Admiralty on the Gulf mine claims; no new word on the frigates; Navy: "On passage. Nothing to add"

*By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent*

First Minister Erik Halvorsen does not like microphones. On Monday, at the Admiralty in Hokelgard, he spoke into one for three minutes about the sea.

Asked about the new mine claim at the Gulf mouth, he stopped at the door.

"A tanker is a small town at sea," he said. "Twenty, thirty people. A galley, a sick bay, a captain who has not slept. When someone says a ship has struck a mine, I think of them first. Then I think of the law."

He went on. "The right to pass through a strait is older than any of the powers now arguing over this one. Our sagas are full of crews who sailed on another's consent and paid the toll. They did not sail on another's whim. The sea does not belong to whoever shouts loudest from the shore. That is the question before us. Our frigates do not sail to argue it. They sail so that others can pass."

He took no other questions.

The Navy gave no fresh word on VNS *Gudrid* and VNS *Kjipuktuk*. "On passage," said Commander Einar Paul, a Navy spokesman. "Nothing to add." The country will be told when the ships arrive. The initial escort period remains four months.

### North-shore power says a tanker struck a mine on the strait's "unauthorised southern route" with its tracking off; monitors cannot confirm; militia kills 51 at a government camp; rates hold

*By Halldor Vik and Elise Brattli*

In the south, the militia that holds the Red Sea narrows attacked a training camp for the regional government's security forces. Local officials said 51 people were killed and many more hurt. The militia also claimed strikes on energy sites and airports in the large Gulf kingdom to its north. None had been confirmed by Monday night.

Karontoborg's war-risk underwriters left their published Gulf rates unchanged. No further Vinlandic-flagged ship switched to the Cape route. The count stays at twenty-three. ## Two Days to the Status Committee, and Now Two Sets of Maps

### Clerk publishes Thursday's order of business: Cree Council first, Skjoldkraft second, Government officials last; Skjoldkraft posts its route maps; Kaninaskum: "Their maps show where the line goes. Ours show what is there"

*By Mary Kanatiio Berg, Constitutional Affairs Correspondent*

The Provincial Thing's status committee on the constitutional peoples meets on Thursday 22 October for its first sitting on the Shield Intertie. On Monday evening the committee clerk published the order of business.

The Kaniapiskau Cree Council will give evidence first. Skjoldkraft, the state power company, follows. Officials from Energy Minister Petter Grimsrud's department come last. Members may question each group in turn.

An hour later, Skjoldkraft posted its route maps and lodged them with the clerk. They show the preferred line across three hundred and ten kilometres of Cree lands, with a wide buffer around the two burial sites. A note says the line "can be routed around both".

A company spokesman said publishing the maps was "the courteous thing to do before we sit in the same room".

Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum spoke by telephone from the north on Monday night. She had seen the maps. "They are good maps," she said. "Their maps show where the line goes. Ours show what is there." She repeated her line of last week: the burial sites are not for trade.

Mr Grimsrud's office said officials would attend "to listen". A status committee does not choose a route. It decides whether the constitutional peoples' status is touched, and on what terms. Under the Restoration of 1652, that question belongs to the Provincial Thing.

# PAGE SIX — SCIENCE & TECHNOLOGY

## Report Stage Today, With a Late Ninety Days

### Government amendment would open the public incident register three months after the law takes effect; Kahente-Moe: "A quarter of a year of incidents nobody can read"; third reading Thursday; checker opens in twelve days

*By Ingvild Sørhus, Technology Correspondent, Karontoborg*

Minister Kari Lindqvist's Autonomous Systems Incident Reporting Bill reaches report stage in the People's Thing this afternoon.

No amendment was lodged before Friday's cut-off. But on Monday evening the Government tabled a late amendment, with the Speaker's leave. The Speaker will decide today whether members may vote on it. It would open the bill's public incident register ninety days after the law takes effect, rather than on the same day.

A ministry spokeswoman called the change "practical". "The register should open with a working form and trained staff," she said, "not as a blank page on the first morning." Operators would still have to report incidents from the first day. The register would publish them later.

Runa Kahente-Moe, Labour's digital affairs spokeswoman, was not persuaded. "Ninety days is a quarter of a year of incidents nobody can read," she said. "If firms must report on day one, the public should read on day one." Labour will oppose the amendment if it is selected.

The Lakes Digital Industry Council welcomed the delay.

The rest of the bill stands as it left committee: a public register, operator liability, and a developer clause held in a six-month review. Depositing counsel still plan a separate motion on a private right of action. They have not tabled it.

Third reading is set for Thursday 22 October, the day the Provincial Thing's status committee sits on the Intertie. A Government whip said the bill "has its votes" at both stages.

Karontoborg's draft contract terms for autonomous systems remain open for comment until the end of the month. The ministry's public checker for machine-made images and audio opens on 1 November, in twelve days. The Laukvik phage preprint still awaits a referee.

## A Vinlandic Counter on the Way to Mars

### Pacific partner's sample-return mission to a Martian moon due to launch today; Stadakona-built dust counter aboard; Space Agency to help track the first days

*By Gunnhild Paquette, Science Correspondent*

Vinland's closest Pacific partner is due to launch a spacecraft today to one of the two moons of Mars. The craft is meant to land, collect soil and bring it back to Earth.

A small Vinlandic instrument is aboard. It is a dust counter about the size of a shoebox, built by a team at Stadakona University. It will count the fine grains that drift around the moon, before the lander touches down and after.

The Vinlandic Space Agency said its Atlantic range station would help track the craft in its first days. The agency's own heavy-lift rocket, Sleipnir-A, reached orbit for the first time on 28 September. Its post-flight review is on schedule.

A Stadakona team member said the group had "built it to wait patiently and then work very fast".

# PAGE NINE — OPINION

## THE HERALD'S VIEW

### Start With the Yards

Treasury did the right thing on Monday, and it did it quickly.

On Sunday the Finance Minister asked the cities to bring serviced land inside twelve months. On Sunday evening the mayors pointed out that some of the best land in the Lakes belongs to the Commonwealth. By Monday afternoon Treasury had promised to publish its own list before the working group meets on 29 October. Each site will carry its holder, its size and any claim against it.

That is how a fair bargain begins. A government cannot ask a city to empty its drawers while its own stay shut. Once the rail yards, port strips and depots are on a public list, the question is no longer whether land exists. It is who builds on it, and on what terms.

Three things should follow. The list must be complete; one that leaves out the awkward sites is a brochure. Every claim by a constitutional people must be marked plainly, as Nora Sakej-Eriksen asks and Treasury now promises. And the working group should settle terms for federal sites before it argues the size of the envelope. Builders are at their gloomiest in a decade. They need land that can be built on, not land that can be quoted.

The Lindqvist bill shows the opposite habit. On Friday no amendment had been lodged. On Monday evening the Government found one, holding back the public incident register for ninety days. The ministry calls this practical. But a bill that asks operators to report on the first day should not ask the public to wait a quarter of a year to read. If members may vote on the change today, they should ask for a fixed opening date instead.

The junior coalition partner has still said nothing. Landvern has no date for the third meeting it promised. The whip says the numbers hold, and today's division will show whether they do. A party in government that cannot say in public what it is doing in private is not being careful. It is waiting to see which way the wind blows.

At sea, the First Minister spoke well. The right of passage is old, and it is not decided by whoever shouts loudest from the shore.

One more thing. These pages have said for several days that the status committee sits on Wednesday 22 October. 22 October is a Thursday. We correct it on page two. A newspaper that asks others to publish their lists should check its own calendar.
