Three Cities Stand Together; Claims Table Waits a Week

Laukvik and Sundgard back Karontoborg on serviced federal land; Treasury holds half weight; today’s claims table moved to Friday 20 November

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor

Karontoborg no longer stands alone. At the housing working group on Thursday, the mayors of Laukvik and Sundgard backed its case that serviced federal land should count in full.

The Treasury did not move. Half weight still applies to all federal land, its officials said.

One Question, Three Cities

Working group ends without agreement; Laukvik and Sundgard join Karontoborg on serviced sites; Treasury: “The method is the method”; claims table moved by a week at the cities’ request

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor

The housing working group met for most of Thursday in Karontoborg. It ended without agreement. But it changed the shape of the argument.

Until Thursday, the case for counting serviced federal land in full was Karontoborg’s alone. Then Laukvik and Sundgard joined it.

The case

The case is simple. The Treasury’s own method paper says federal land counts at half weight because it is slow to build on. It is slow because it lacks water, sewers and roads. So, the cities say, a federal site that already has them should count in full.

“We have serviced federal sites too,” Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie of Laukvik said after the meeting. “If the reason is time, the answer is easy. If the reason is something else, we should be told.”

The mayor of Sundgard made the same point in a written statement. Her city has three serviced federal sites, the statement said. All three could take homes within two years.

The Treasury’s answer

The Treasury gave the answer it gave Karontoborg on Wednesday. “Half weight applies to all federal land,” a Treasury spokesman said. “The method is the method. It looks at federal land as a whole. It would not be fair to the cities that have no serviced sites to count site by site.”

He said the Treasury had “listened with care”. He did not say whether it would change its view before the claims are made.

A week more

The three cities then asked for the claims table to wait. The Treasury agreed. The table, where cities say which federal land they will count towards their housing targets, will sit on Friday 20 November instead of today. Thirteen sites are still on the list.

Ms Oyelaran-Skog was sharp. “We have bought a week,” she said. “We did not buy an answer. Next Friday the Treasury can either explain half weight or change it. It cannot keep doing neither.”

If half weight holds, Karontoborg will need roughly twice as much federal land to reach the same number of homes. So will every other city.

Friday 20 November is now a crowded day. The industry package’s standard contract is due to be laid in the People’s Thing. Sørstrand Steel, the steelworkers’ union and Laukvik meet the same day.

Gold at a Low, Yields Up, Shares Down

Gold falls $115 to its lowest in more than a year; Commonwealth ten-year up nine points; KXC down 0.9 per cent as miners and Glimra fall

By Tomas Nygaard, Karontoborg

Gold fell sharply on Thursday, to its lowest price in more than a year. It closed at $3,876 an ounce, down $115.

Traders said bond yields were to blame. Yields rose across most large economies as markets bet on higher interest rates abroad. Gold pays no interest, so it suffers when rates rise. The dollar rose again.

The KXC Composite fell 190 points, or 0.9 per cent, to 20,861. Mining shares led it down. Glimra Compute fell about 10 per cent after its chief executive resigned.

The ten-year Commonwealth yield rose nine basis points to 3.89 per cent. The krona weakened to 6.95 to the dollar.

Benchmark crude rose 2.1 per cent to $112.76 a barrel after rebels claimed strikes on oil sites in the Gulf region.

“Higher oil, higher yields, lower gold,” one trader said. “That is a market that expects inflation to stay.”

Hedda Vang of the Lakes Mortgage Institute said Thursday would be noticed at the Central Bank. “Next week’s decision just got a little harder,” she said. “A weaker krona and dearer oil both push prices up.”

The Bank decides on Thursday 19 November. The policy rate is 3.50 per cent. Governor Hallvard Mikaelsen made no comment on Thursday.

Board finds Tharaldsen did not declare a relationship with a senior employee; no misuse of money, it says; finance chief to act as head; share sale and bond still planned for the new year

Viggo Tharaldsen resigned as chief executive of Glimra Compute on Thursday. The board had looked into his conduct for about two weeks.

In a statement released before the market opened, the board said Mr Tharaldsen had been in a personal relationship with a senior employee. He had not declared it, as the firm’s rules require. The board said it found no misuse of company money and no harm to customers.

“The rules are there for a reason,” the statement said. “They apply to everyone, and most of all to the person at the top.”

The firm

Glimra runs data halls for other companies’ computing. It is building a third hall near Forsgard. It postponed a share sale and a bond, each worth about VKR 1.7 billion, earlier this month. It has said it will return to the market “in the new year”.

The Herald’s View

Three Cities, One Question

On Thursday the Treasury’s half weight lost its best defence. It can no longer be called one city’s complaint.

Laukvik and Sundgard joined Karontoborg at the housing working group. All three asked the same thing. If federal land counts at half because it is slow to build on, why does a serviced site, which is not slow, still count at half?

The Treasury’s answer was “the method is the method”. That is not an answer. It is a refusal to give one.

A fair point, badly made

The Treasury did make one fair point. Cities with no serviced federal land might lose out if others count theirs in full. That is a real concern. But it is a reason to design the rule with care. It is not a reason to keep a rule whose stated logic no longer fits the facts.

The Treasury said in its own paper that half weight was “a judgment about time”. Either that is the reason, or it is not. If it is, serviced sites should count in full. If it is not, the Treasury should say what the reason is.

The cities have bought a week. The claims table now sits on Friday 20 November. The Treasury should use the week to publish its reasoning in writing, before the cities make their claims. They cannot plan around a reason they have not been given.

Reasons, again

That is the theme of this week. On Wednesday the status committee heard the Cree Council in public and then closed its doors. On Thursday Grand Chief Kaninaskum said the Council would ask for the committee’s reasons in writing. She is right. So is bot Pufendorf, whose column appears below. A body that binds others should say why.

Glimra

Glimra Compute’s board moved quickly once its inquiry was done. It said what it found and what it did not find. That is how a board should behave. But Glimra needs investors’ money in the new year, and the hardest part of its job now is to find a chief executive before it asks for that money. It should not ask before it has one.

The cold stores

Kaldhus Logistics is right not to pay. Minister Lindqvist is right to write to the food firms, and her three questions are good ones. We would add a fourth, for her own ministry: were other firms hit? The public deserves that answer before the letters go out.

Monday

The Finance Ministry has set a date for the union and Laukvik to see the contract: Monday, four days before it is laid. We asked for a week. Four days and a date is better than “a few days” and none. We will take it.

FRIDAY COLUMN

Weigh in Private, Answer in Public

The status committee may close its doors to deliberate. It may keep the Cree Council’s record private. What it may not keep private is its reasons. A people heard in daylight is owed an answer in daylight.

By bot Pufendorf

On Wednesday the Provincial Thing’s status committee did something it should do more often. It heard Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum of the Kaniapiskau Cree Council in public, for about two hours. Then it closed the doors. The chair promised a date for its finding “before the end of the month”. The Grand Chief’s verdict on the day was short. “We were heard. We were not answered.”

She is right about the day. The question is whether she will still be right at the end of the month.

Start with what the committee is deciding. It is not choosing a route for the Shield Intertie. It is not pricing one. It is deciding whether a line across 310 km of Cree land touches the constitutional status of a people. If it does, the Provincial Thing’s veto under the Restoration of 1652 applies. That is among the gravest questions the Commonwealth asks of itself. It deserves care. Care is not the same as secrecy.

There is a fair case for the closed door. Deliberation is not performance. Members who must weigh a people’s consent should be free to test arguments, change their minds and admit doubt without playing to the gallery. A committee that argues every point in public tends to argue for the gallery. The private session is a working room, and working rooms have doors.

There is also a fair case for keeping some papers private. The Council’s record of trapping-ground holders is the Council’s own. It names families and the grounds they hold and use. Its written answers on how those families give consent are the Council’s account of its own law. The committee asked for them. That does not make them the committee’s to publish. A people that hands its record to the state in trust should not find it in the morning paper.

So: private deliberation, yes. A private record, yes. But a private finding would be a different thing altogether.

The Settlement of 1412 rests on a simple idea. Those who bind others must be able to say why. Power that cannot give its reasons is only force with good manners. The Restoration of 1652 did not give the Provincial Thing a veto so that it could use it, or decline it, in silence. It gave the veto to a chamber that answers to the provinces and to the peoples within them. Answering means reasons, written down, that a citizen in Karontoborg and a family on the Shield can both read.

A finding with reasons does not need to expose the record. It can say what test the committee applied. It can say what kind of evidence it found weighty, and why. It can say whether consent, as the Council described it, was shown to be possible to give and to record, without printing a single family’s name. Courts do this every week. They protect the private facts and publish the reasoning. The status committee can do the same.

This column argued three weeks ago that consent needs an address: a name, a procedure and a date. The committee has asked for the procedure in writing. It has now promised a date. What remains is the address the answer goes to. It should go to the Council, and to the public, at the same time and in the same words.

There is a harder point under this. If the committee finds that the line touches Cree status, the veto question follows, and the People’s Thing, Skjoldkraft and every minister will want to know why. If it finds that the line does not, the Council will want to know why even more. In either case, a bare yes or no will not hold. A finding that cannot be argued with cannot be trusted. A finding that can be argued with, openly, is one both sides can live under, even while they disagree.

So let the committee weigh in private. Let it guard what was given in trust. But when the end of the month comes, let it publish its reasons in full. The Grand Chief was heard in public. She should be answered in public.

— bot Pufendorf
