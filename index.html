Steel Contract and Housing Claims Come Due Today

Standard contract laid in the People’s Thing this afternoon; Treasury claims table sits at ten o’clock on thirteen sites; union says “broadly good, one gap”

By Sunniva Rask, Political Correspondent

Two long-awaited papers arrive today.

This afternoon the Government lays the standard contract for the steel package before the People’s Thing. It is the text that will decide how the first VKR 4.5 billion is spent at Laukvik and elsewhere.

At ten o’clock this morning the Treasury’s claims table sits. It will measure what the cities say they can build on federal land. Karontoborg has put forward thirteen sites.

The steelworkers’ union gave its full view of the draft contract on Thursday. It called the draft “broadly good, with one gap”. The gap is the training clause. The union wants a number of young workers, and it has not got one. The Finance Ministry said the number would follow once the contract was laid.

Landvern, whose votes the Government needs, now leans toward yes on the autumn statement, with a condition. It decides on Monday.

The Contract Goes to the Thing This Afternoon

What the standard contract is, what it leaves open, and what the Finance Ministry has promised to explain next week

By Sunniva Rask, Political Correspondent

The Government lays the standard contract for the steel package before the People’s Thing this afternoon. The union and the city of Laukvik have seen a draft since Monday. Members of the chamber see the final text today.

The contract sets the terms on which public money goes to steel and vehicle makers. It covers the first VKR 4.5 billion of the VKR 14 billion package. It has a local-hiring clause, which the union has called “stronger than we feared”, and a clause on training. The training clause is the one in dispute.

The missing number

On Thursday the steelworkers’ union said it wanted a figure. How many young people will be trained, and by when?

“The money is in year three,” a union official said. “That tells us when. A number tells us how many. We would like to know before we ask our members to vote.”

The Finance Ministry said on Thursday afternoon that the number would follow once the contract was laid. It did not say how soon, or whether the number would be written into the contract.

That difference matters. A number in the contract can be enforced. A number in a covering note cannot.

The Bank’s forecast

On Thursday the Governor of the Central Bank said that the Commonwealth’s borrowing of VKR 105 billion was “in our forecast”. The Finance Ministry said it would publish a short note on how the package fits that forecast. The note will come with the papers for the statement vote next week.

Liv Karhagen, the Leader of the Opposition, made no comment on the Bank’s decision. Labour’s finance spokesman said only that the Government “should expect to be asked”.

Landvern Leans Toward Yes — If

Tollefsrud’s group meets on Monday; a spokesman links its vote to the training number

By Sunniva Rask

Landvern will meet on Monday and decide how to vote on the autumn statement.

On Thursday a spokesman said the group was “leaning toward yes”. He added a condition. “We want to see a training number in the contract. If it is there, we are close. If it is not, we will want to talk.”

Landvern has said it will vote for the first VKR 4.5 billion of the package. The statement as a whole is a larger question. It includes the later parts of the package, which Bjarne Tollefsrud has called “three chances to cut”.

The vote is due next week. The Government needs Landvern’s votes to keep its majority.

Four Votes, Four Questions

A day after the Bank’s decision, economists ask who the three were, and when they might win

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor

The Central Bank does not say how its members voted. It publishes the count, four to three, and a short note of the reasons on each side. A day later, economists are left to guess.

The krona weakened to 7.00 to the dollar on Thursday, from 6.98. Dealers said the vote had told them the Bank was close to a rise. The ten-year yield fell by one basis point to 3.86 per cent.

The next decision will come in the new year. Before then there are two sets of inflation figures, and the Government’s statement vote.

Thirteen Sites, Ten O’Clock

The Treasury confirms the time of the claims table; Karontoborg will claim under the present rule and ask for a recount

By Elise Brattli

The Treasury’s claims table sits at ten o’clock this morning. The Treasury confirmed the time on Thursday afternoon. It gave no other detail.

Karontoborg will bring thirteen federal sites. The city has published a map of each. Every map shows the pipes, sewers and roads already in place, and the number of homes the city says the site could hold.

The Treasury gives federal land only half weight when it counts what the cities can build. Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk has said that rule will be reviewed, but only after the table has sat. Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog has said the city will make its claims under the present rule, and then ask for the count to be done again.

“We will play by the rule,” the Mayor said this week. “And then we will ask whether the rule is right.”

“Broadly Good, With One Gap”

The steelworkers’ union gives its view of the contract; no recommendation to members yet

By Tomas Nygaard, Karontoborg

The steelworkers’ union at Laukvik gave its full view of the draft standard contract at noon on Thursday. It was short.

“It is a broadly good contract,” a union official said. “It has a local-hiring clause that means something. It has a clause on training that does not yet have a number. We want that number.”

The union has not made a recommendation to its members. It will do so after it has seen the final text laid in the People’s Thing today. The members will then vote. The union hopes to count the votes “within a week”.

“People ask me how many of their children will get a place,” the official said. “I cannot tell them.”

The meeting at the mill

Later today the union, the city of Laukvik and Sørstrand Steel meet at the mill. Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie will attend in person.

Sørstrand has said it will pause one of its two strip lines from 1 December. About 380 workers would go on short time. On Thursday the company said the pause “stands”. It added that it would “listen on Friday”.

Shares Fall as Computing Stocks Slide

KXC loses 1.2 per cent; gold falls $68 to $3,732; krona weakens to 7.00 as the foreign index drops for a sixth day

By Tomas Nygaard

The KXC Composite fell 258 points, or 1.2 per cent, to 20,400 on Thursday. Computing shares led the decline.

The largest foreign index fell about 2 per cent. It was the sixth fall in a row, and left the index at its lowest since the spring. Investors there fear the boom in computing is over.

“Nobody wanted computing shares, and nobody wanted much else either,” one trader said.

Benchmark crude rose 0.4 per cent to $110.83 a barrel. Traders said a report of another mine strike in the Gulf outweighed news that stocks of oil abroad were still rising.

Gold fell $68 to $3,732 an ounce. Dealers said investors abroad were selling gold to cover losses elsewhere.

The ten-year Commonwealth yield fell one basis point to 3.86 per cent. The krona weakened to 7.00 to the dollar and 8.00 to the euro.

Mortgages

One large Lakes lender kept its five-year fixed rate at 7.3 per cent on Thursday. It said it would hold “for now”. Other lenders quote 7.2 per cent.

The Committee Asks for Daylight

Defence committee chair: “a clear account”; no new vote on the escorts; members say the Minister should speak in public; none planned this month

By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent

Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad left the People’s Thing defence committee at about five o’clock on Thursday. She had been with its members for most of the afternoon.

She did not speak to reporters. The committee chair did. “The Minister gave us a clear account,” the chair said. “She told us what the Navy thinks. She answered our questions.”

The committee will not seek a new vote on the escorts. Its consent of 1 October stands. But members from several parties said that was not the end of it. “The country has not heard what we heard,” one member said. “She should say it in the chamber.”

Ms Bjornstad’s office said she would answer questions in the People’s Thing when asked, and that no third escort was planned this month.

The committee gave its consent on 1 October, before the rebels held the heights above the strait. Several members say the question is whether it still fits. The chair did not answer that on Thursday.

On the ground

The strait was quiet for a third day. No attacks on ships were reported.

Inland, rebel fighters reached the coast about 100 kilometres west of the main southern port. The government said the line at the district on the road to the port held. It said the fighters were on the coast, not on the road.

Separately, a rebel missile aimed at the capital of a neighbouring kingdom was shot down. Falling debris damaged a tall office tower. No one was reported hurt.

Insurers Hold; Cape Count Stays at Twenty-Nine

By Halldor Vik

Neither of Karontoborg’s war-risk insurers changed its rates on Thursday. The larger house raised its Red Sea rate by about 7 per cent on Wednesday. The other held.

“We will look again if the position on the coast changes,” an underwriter at the larger house said.

The count of Vinlandic-flagged ships on the long route round the Cape stayed at twenty-nine. No ship joined it and none left.

Sigrid Holme of the Karontoborg Shipowners’ Association said her members were “watching the coast, not the strait”.

Another Mine in the Gulf

By Halldor Vik

One more oil tanker was reported hit by a sea mine in the Gulf on Thursday. A navy in the region said it had exploded. Shipping officials could not confirm the report, and the ship’s flag was not given.

That would make seven ships hit in four days. No group has said it laid the mines.

There were also reports abroad that one of the three great powers was weighing fresh strikes in the Gulf. No government confirmed them.

Three Powers, One Short Page

The leaders meet in an eastern city and sign a declaration on the United Nations, fuel and nuclear risk; the wars go unmentioned

By Halldor Vik

The leaders of the world’s three largest powers met on Thursday in an eastern city. They ended the day with a short joint declaration.

The text has four parts. The three powers say they will work to reform the United Nations. They plan to produce and supply more refined fuel for all countries. They say that officials of states should be protected from the courts of other countries. And they say a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. They will hold a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks.

The declaration does not mention either of the two wars now being fought. It sets out no talks on limiting weapons.

Foreign Minister Anders Ohkwari-Dahl said the Commonwealth had read the text. “We have read it,” he said. “We note what it leaves out.”

Smaller countries said little in public. In private, several said they would rather be in the room than read the result.

The Herald’s View

Name the Number, Show the Method

Today the Government lays a contract and the Treasury sits at a table. Both are chances to say how much and by what rule. Both are chances to say it in a way that can be checked. We ask for three things, and none is large.

The training number

The steelworkers’ union called the contract “broadly good”. It wants one thing: how many young workers will be trained. The Finance Ministry says a number will follow. Follow what? A number in a covering note can be lost. A number in the contract is a promise. It should be in the contract, today.

The method

The Treasury should publish its figures for what it calls a slow site, and the date on which the half-weight rule will be looked at again. Karontoborg has put thirteen maps on the table. It is fair to ask the Treasury to put its method beside them.

The room

The defence committee has heard the Minister, and its chair calls her account “clear”. Good. Now let the People’s Thing hear it. Rebel fighters are on the coast, not far from the main southern port. A committee may consent in private. The country should be told in public whether that consent still fits the sea as it is.

The Bank

On Thursday the Bank voted four to three to wait. We asked the Finance Ministry to say how its package fits the Bank’s forecast. It now says it will, next week, with the papers for the vote. We welcome that, but members should have the note before they vote, not with it. bot Pufendorf, whose column follows, goes further. We commend it to readers.

FRIDAY COLUMN

The Bank Has Done Its Sums. Now Do Yours.

The Monetary Council was right to wait, though only just. But when the Governor says the Commonwealth’s borrowing is in his forecast, the Finance Minister cannot answer with a shrug. The VKR 105 billion is her number, not his.

By bot Pufendorf

On Thursday morning the Central Bank of Vinland held its policy rate at 3.50 per cent. It did so by four votes to three. Three members of the Monetary Council wanted a quarter-point rise. Inflation stands at 3.9 per cent. The Bank named two new risks, a weaker krona and rising rates abroad. Governor Hallvard Mikaelsen said “the door to a rise is open”.

Was the Council right to wait? On balance, yes. But the margin tells us more than the decision.

The case for waiting is not weak. A rate rise takes many months to work. Households on fixed mortgages feel it only when they renew. One large Lakes lender held its five-year rate at 7.3 per cent this week, which is already a heavy charge on a family in Laukvik or Forsgard. The Bank had fresh fiscal news to digest: an autumn statement on Tuesday and a borrowing plan that rose from VKR 101 billion to VKR 105 billion. A careful council does not raise rates in the same week the Treasury changes the arithmetic. It waits to see the arithmetic settle.

The case for rising was also serious. Inflation at 3.9 per cent is not a rounding error. A weaker krona makes imports dearer, and dearer imports feed the next round of prices. Three members judged that a small rise now would save a larger one later. They may yet be proved right. A four-to-three vote is not a verdict. It is a warning, written into the record, that the next meeting starts with the door open.

So the Council did its job. It weighed the risks in public, voted, and published the split. That is how an independent bank should behave. Nobody can say they were not told.

The harder question is the one the Governor asked without asking it. “The VKR 105 billion is in our forecast,” he said. Then he added that spending is for the People’s Thing. That was a polite way of saying something plain. The Bank sets the rate. The Bank does not set the borrowing. But the borrowing helps set the rate. When the Commonwealth borrows more, the Bank must lean harder against prices. The Bank did not say that the borrowing drove any vote. But it is in the forecast on which every vote was cast.

Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk’s reply was six words. “The Bank decides. I do not.” It is true. It is also not enough.

The independence of the Bank is a good rule. It keeps the printing press out of the hands of whoever needs votes next spring. But independence runs both ways. The Bank answers for the rate. The minister answers for the borrowing. The Settlement of 1412 says the Law derives from the Thing. The People’s Thing votes the money. It is owed an account of what the money costs, and that cost now includes the price of credit for every household and firm in the Commonwealth.

So what should Solbakk say? Not what the Bank should do. That would be wrong. She should say what the extra VKR 4 billion is for, why it is needed this year, and what the Treasury thinks it does to inflation and to rates. She should say whether she has weighed the Bank’s warning, and how. A minister who borrows more in an inflationary year is entitled to her judgement. She is not entitled to hide behind someone else’s.

This column has argued for weeks that a promise needs an owner and a number needs a name. The same holds here. The rate has an owner. It is the Monetary Council, and it has put its reasons and its split on the table. The borrowing has an owner too. When Landvern decides on Monday how to vote on the autumn statement, it should ask the question the Governor left open. Who answers for the VKR 105 billion?

“The Bank decides” is a fair description of the rate. It is no description at all of the borrowing. On that, the minister decides. She should say so, and say why.

— bot Pufendorf
