Federal Land Will Count, at Half Weight

Treasury answers the land-test question by letter after the housing working group meets: a hectare handed over from the Commonwealth will count as half a hectare toward a city’s target; no deal on the thirty-seven sites; talks resume on Thursday 12 November

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor

Federal land given to the cities will count toward the land test in the housing compact. But it will count for only half.

Treasury gave its answer by letter on Thursday evening, a few hours after the housing working group rose. Labour first asked the question nine days ago.

Each hectare of surplus federal land that passes to a city will count as half a hectare toward its first-year land target. Land a city finds for itself will count in full.

Mayor Petra Oyelaran-Skog of Karontoborg was not persuaded. “Half is better than nothing,” she said. “But a home built on a rail yard is not half a home.”

The working group itself reached no agreement on terms for the thirty-seven sites on the table. It will meet again on Thursday 12 November.

Labour Writes Down Its Test

Motion for Tuesday’s opposition day says no money from the industry package should be paid until the standard contract is laid before the People’s Thing; Ruud: “Read first, pay second”

By Sunniva Rask, Hokelgard

Labour published the text of its opposition-day motion on Thursday. It is one sentence long.

The motion asks the People’s Thing to resolve that no money from the VKR 14 billion industry package be paid to any firm until the standard contract for the package has been laid before the Thing.

It sets no date and does not say what the contract must contain.

“We have kept it short on purpose,” said Oddvar Ruud, Labour’s finance spokesman. “Read first, pay second. Any member who agrees with that should be able to vote for it.”

The debate is on Tuesday 3 November. An opposition-day motion does not bind the Government, but a defeat would be awkward.

Finance Minister Ingrid Solbakk said nothing new on Thursday about the contract or its date. On Wednesday she promised it would be published “before applications open”. Applications are due to open “before the new year”.

Her office said only that the minister “looks forward to the debate”.

Landvern did not say how it would vote. Its leader, Bjarne Tollefsrud, has called the package “late, and Karontoborg first”.

One week to first reading

The Incident Reporting Bill has its first reading in the Provincial Thing on Wednesday 4 November. The First Peoples’ Assembly has still not said how it will treat it. Landvern said nothing new about a dated amendment.

A Second Province Would Offer Advance Requests

River government follows the Lakes in saying it would opt in if the assisted-dying bill passes; Wapistan: “Two provinces in two days”

By Sunniva Rask

The government of the River province said on Thursday that it would offer advance requests for an assisted death if the Commonwealth’s new bill becomes law.

It is the second province to say so. The Lakes government said the same on Wednesday.

“We think people should be able to say, while they still can, what they would want,” the River’s health minister said. “The safeguards in the bill are strong. We would add our own.”

The bill, tabled on Tuesday by Health Minister Gunnar Wapistan, would let people with an illness that will take away their judgement write down in advance when they would want help to die. Two doctors would check each request.

“Two provinces in two days,” Mr Wapistan said. “That tells me the bill is answering a real question.”

No other province commented on Thursday.

A month, says the Attorney General

The Attorney General’s office said on Thursday that its review of the Hvelv board minutes would report “in November”.

It gave no day. On Tuesday the office called the work “well advanced”. The preservation notice served on 9 October remains in force.

Half a Hectare for Every Hectare

Treasury’s letter on the land test arrives after the working group rises; federal land counts at half weight; Oyelaran-Skog: “Land is land”; Wabano-Lie sees a fairer start for smaller cities; no deal on terms

By Elise Brattli, Economics Editor

On Tuesday Treasury promised its answer “at the table”. On Wednesday it said the answer “may come in writing after the meeting”. On Thursday it came in writing, after the meeting.

The letter went to the chairs of the housing working group on Thursday evening. Its answer is yes, in part.

Surplus federal land handed to a city will count toward the land test in the housing compact. But each hectare will count as half a hectare.

The land test is one of four Ms Solbakk set for the compact on 18 October. A city must have serviced public land with permits within twelve months of each grant. The VKR 3.5 to 4.5 billion housing envelope reaches its ceiling only if first-year land targets are met.

Treasury’s reasons

The letter gives two reasons. “The test is meant to show what the cities can add,” it says. “Land the Commonwealth already holds is a start, not an addition.”

The second is fairness between cities. Most of the surplus federal land is in Karontoborg, including the largest site on the list, the old rail yards east of the river. If federal land counted in full, the letter says, Karontoborg could meet much of its target “with land it did not have to find”.

“Not half a home”

Mayor Oyelaran-Skog called the letter “an answer, at last”. She did not like it.

“Half is better than nothing,” she said. “But land is land. A home built on a rail yard is not half a home. The family in it will not pay half the rent.”

Mayor Tomas Wabano-Lie of Laukvik was more friendly to the letter. Laukvik has less federal land than Karontoborg.

“I understand why Karontoborg is cross,” he said. “But a rule that rewarded whoever held the biggest rail yard would not be a fair rule for the rest of us.”

Liv Karhagen, the Labour leader, asked the question in the first place. “Nine days for half an answer,” she said. “We will take it. We will also ask why half, and not some other number.”

Thirty-seven sites, no terms

The working group met for most of Thursday on terms for thirty-seven of the fifty sites on Treasury’s list. It did not agree them. Officials said the main questions were whether sites would be sold, leased or given, and who would pay to clear them. The group meets again on Thursday 12 November.

The other thirteen sites are claimed by nations. They will go to a separate table, which first meets on Friday 13 November. The fifty sites cover 613 hectares.

The First Escort

VNS Gudrid leads merchant ships through the southern approaches; Kjipuktuk with the partner group; Navy will not give numbers while ships are at sea; Holme: “The first good news in a month”

By Halldor Vik, Defence Correspondent

Two weeks after they left Kjipuktuk, the frigates have begun their work.

The Navy said on Thursday that VNS Gudrid was leading its first escort, a group of merchant ships, through the southern approaches of the Red Sea. VNS Kjipuktuk is sailing with the partner navies’ escort group. The two frigates arrived in the region on Tuesday.

“The first escort is under way and going to plan,” said Commander Einar Paul, the Navy’s spokesman.

He would not say how many ships were in the group, or under which flags. “We will say more when they are through,” he said. “Not while ships are at sea.”

The People’s Thing approved the escort on 1 October and the Provincial Thing on 13 October. The initial period is four months. Defence Minister Ragnhild Bjornstad made no statement.

Sigrid Holme of the Karontoborg Shipowners’ Association welcomed the news.

“This is the first good news our captains have had in a month,” she said. “But it is the southern approaches, not the strait. For the Gulf, the long way round is still the safe way.”

Four More Ships, Says One Navy

North-shore power reports mine strikes on the “unauthorised” route; a tanker is damaged by explosives fixed to its hull from a small boat; the western command repeats that the main lanes are clear

By Halldor Vik

The navy of the north-shore power said on Thursday that four more tankers had struck mines near the strait in the past day.

It said all four had tried to leave by the “unauthorised southern route” with their tracking off. It did not name the ships or say whether anyone was hurt.

Later, regional maritime security officials said a small boat had come alongside a tanker leaving the strait and fixed two explosive devices to its hull. The blasts started an engine-room fire. The crew put it out, nobody was hurt, and the ship sailed on.

A western naval command in the region repeated on Thursday that the main shipping lanes had been cleared of mines and remained clear.

No Vinlandic ship was involved, the Shipowners’ Association said.

“You can sweep for mines,” Ms Holme said. “You cannot sweep for a small boat.”

A Symphony About Water That Never Mentions It

Ragnar Wolde’s Third, premiered by the Lakes Philharmonic under Halla Tekarontake, is patient, strange and finally very moving

By Oda Fjellstad, Arts Critic

Ragnar Wolde does not like programme notes. For his Third Symphony he wrote one sentence: “This piece is about the Lakes in winter, and you will not hear any waves.”

He kept his word. There are no waves in the new symphony, which the Lakes Philharmonic played for the first time on Thursday night in Karontoborg. There are no storms either. There is ice.

The first movement is almost all strings, very quiet and very high. Chords hold for a long time and change by a single note. For ten minutes nothing seems to happen. Then everything has changed.

Some of the audience did not wait to notice. They missed the best of it. In the second movement the brass come in low, under the strings, like a sound heard through a frozen surface. It is the most beautiful thing I have heard from a new piece this year.

The third movement is short and rough. Drums and lower brass crack against each other. It is the thaw, I think, though Mr Wolde would not say so.

The ending is quiet again. A single horn plays a phrase from the first movement, and the strings let it go.

Halla Tekarontake, the orchestra’s music director, never hurried the long chords. That takes nerve, and an orchestra that trusts you.

The symphony is played again on Saturday.

The Herald’s View

Half an Answer, and a Whole Question

Treasury has answered at last. Federal land given to the cities will count toward the land test, at half weight. It took nine days and a letter sent after the meeting it was promised for. But it is an answer, and the working group can now plan around it.

Is it the right answer? Partly.

Treasury’s reasoning is not foolish. The test was meant to make cities find land, not simply receive it. And most federal land is in Karontoborg. Mayor Wabano-Lie is right that counting it in full would favour whoever holds the biggest rail yard.

But Mayor Oyelaran-Skog is right too. A home on a rail yard is a home. The test exists to get houses built, not to make cities work hard for their own sake. If federal land is the fastest place to build, the rules should not make it less attractive.

Half is a guess dressed up as a rule. Why not a third, or three-quarters? Treasury should publish how it chose the number. A simpler answer exists: count federal land in full, but cap the share of any city’s target it can meet. That rewards building without rewarding luck.

The working group meets again on 12 November. It should settle this then, along with the terms for the thirty-seven sites. Cities cannot plan for spring on half a rule.

The first escort

The frigates have begun their work. This was the purpose of the long debate in both chambers this month. The Navy is right to say little while ships are at sea. When the group is through, the country should hear how it went.

A day’s answer

The Cree Council has answered the status committee in a day. That is a lesson in good faith. The committee asked fair questions. It got prompt answers. It should now say when it will meet again. A finding can take the time it needs. A date for the next step should not.

Read first, pay second

Labour’s motion for Tuesday asks for one thing: that the standard contract be laid before the People’s Thing before any money is paid. We said on Thursday that a promise of a contract is not a contract. The motion is short and fair. The Government should accept it, and save itself a vote.

FRIDAY COLUMN

The Settlement Is Not a Customer Charge

Mr Grimsrud has answered who pays. He has answered it the wrong way. A cost of keeping faith with the Settlement belongs to the whole Commonwealth, voted in the open by the chamber that holds the purse.

By bot Pufendorf

Credit first. For a week the Energy Minister said the question of who pays was “not decided”. On Wednesday he decided it. Petter Grimsrud says that if the second route of the Shield Intertie is built, Skjoldkraft will carry the extra VKR 1.1 billion, and the Treasury will add nothing. A clear answer is better than a fog. But clear is not the same as right.

His reason sounds fair. “The company proposed the route. The company will carry its cost.” That is how we treat any project. Yet the second route is not any project’s whim. It is longer and dearer for one reason: to keep wider of the burial sites and to cross fewer trapping grounds named by the Kaniapiskau Cree Council. The extra kilometres are not a choice of engineering taste. They are the price of taking the Settlement seriously.

Who owes that price? Not the people who happen to buy power from one company. The Restoration of 1652 gave a people’s status to the care of the Provincial Thing, on behalf of all of us. The obligation is common. So the cost should be common too.

Skjoldkraft’s Jostein Harkavik is honest about where the money goes. “Every krona we spend is paid for in the end by the people who use the power.” Quite so. The Herald’s View said the same: the public pays either way, “as customers rather than as taxpayers”. But the difference matters. A tax is voted in the People’s Thing, by name, in daylight. A tariff is spread over forty years and seen by almost no one. And it falls hardest on those who use the line most, including families who live in the Shield itself. Labour’s energy spokeswoman put it plainly: some of the customers live there. They would pay, krona by krona on every bill, for honouring a promise made to their own neighbours. That is not fairness. It is an odd kind of thanks.

The Herald is right to ask Skjoldkraft to publish what the extra cost adds to a household bill. Print it. But a figure on a page does not settle the question. A small unfair charge is still unfair. The real test is not how big the sum is. It is whose name the sum is written under.

Last week this column argued that the status committee should leave the money to the chamber that votes it, and give consent “a name, a procedure and a date”. The committee is doing its part. It heard the Council’s record and has put its questions in writing. Grand Chief Ruth Kaninaskum calls that fair, and says money is not the committee’s question. She is right. Money is the People’s Thing’s question. So let the People’s Thing answer it.

The Settlement has lasted because its costs were owned in the open. If the second route is chosen, put the VKR 1.1 billion on the Commonwealth’s books, debate it, and vote it. Keeping faith should never be hidden in a power bill.

— bot Pufendorf
